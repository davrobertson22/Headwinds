How Airline Alliances Work

Most of the world's big airlines belong to one of three alliances: Star Alliance, Oneworld or SkyTeam. This article explains what they share, how codeshares and joint ventures fit in, why regulators watch them closely, and how alliances work in Headwinds, where every member is a real player.

By the Headwinds team · Last updated November 12, 2026

What an alliance is, and what it is not

An airline alliance is a long-term agreement between airlines that stay separate companies. Each keeps its own owners, fleet, staff and brand. What they share is the passenger's journey: they sell seats on each other's flights, connect their schedules at their hubs, honour each other's frequent flyer programmes and open their lounges to each other's best customers. A merger joins two airlines into one. An alliance lets them behave a little like one while remaining two.

The reason is reach. No airline can fly everywhere, and many countries limit what foreign airlines may do in their markets. A passenger flying from a small American city to a small Asian one can buy a single ticket that uses three airlines, with bags checked through, because those airlines belong to the same alliance.

How the alliances formed

The first large partnership was between Northwest Airlines and KLM, which began codesharing on a large scale in 1989. In 1992 the Netherlands signed the first "open skies" agreement with the United States, and the US gave the Northwest–KLM partnership immunity from antitrust law in return. That immunity, which let the two airlines plan schedules and fares together, became the model for what followed.

The global alliances came in quick succession. Star Alliance was founded on 14 May 1997 by Air Canada, Lufthansa, Scandinavian Airlines, Thai Airways and United. Oneworld was unveiled in September 1998 and began operating on 1 February 1999, founded by American Airlines, British Airways, Canadian Airlines, Cathay Pacific and Qantas. SkyTeam followed on 22 June 2000, founded by Delta, Aeroméxico, Air France and Korean Air.

Membership moves. Scandinavian Airlines, a Star Alliance founder, left on 31 August 2024 and joined SkyTeam the next day. ITA Airways left SkyTeam in April 2025 and joined Star Alliance in 2026. Virgin Atlantic joined SkyTeam in March 2023 as its first British member. Airlines switch alliances when their partners change: a new shareholder, a merger, or a joint venture with a member of another alliance.

What members actually share

Codeshares

A codeshare lets one airline sell seats on another airline's flight under its own flight number. The airline flying the aircraft is the operating carrier, and the others selling the seat are marketing carriers. The term was coined in 1989 by Qantas and American Airlines, though an American commuter airline had sold its flights under a larger carrier's code as early as 1967. For the airlines, a codeshare puts their flight numbers on routes they do not fly. For passengers it means one ticket, coordinated connections and frequent flyer miles on the whole trip. Consumer groups have long complained that it can hide which airline is actually flying.

Loyalty, lounges and connections

Alliance members let frequent flyers earn and spend miles across each other's flights, and give top-tier members lounge access and priority handling on any member airline. They share terminals, check-in and staff where it saves money, and buy supplies and services together for larger discounts. Round-the-world tickets exist because of alliances: no single airline could offer one.

Joint ventures: the layer underneath

The deepest cooperation happens in joint ventures, which go much further than an alliance. Airlines in a joint venture plan capacity together on a set of routes and share the revenue, so that it no longer matters to them whose aircraft a passenger flies on. That arrangement is called metal neutrality, and regulators make it a condition of the antitrust immunity that lets the partners discuss fares and capacity at all. Three such joint ventures, one built around each alliance, dominate the North Atlantic: by 2017 they controlled nearly 70% of the seats between North America and Europe, the Middle East and Africa.

The case against

Alliances and joint ventures are partly agreements not to compete. Where two members both flew a route, an alliance can mean higher fares and fewer flights than the two airlines offered separately, and the effect is strongest between hubs, such as Detroit and Amsterdam in the Northwest–KLM era. That is why the deepest arrangements need antitrust immunity, and why regulators can attach conditions to them. Supporters answer that passengers gain connections and through-fares no single airline could offer.

Alliances in Headwinds

Headwinds is a multiplayer airline game in which every rival airline is run by a real person, so there are no preset blocs. Alliances are founded by players, from the Alliances tab, and the founder approves or turns down each request to join. What they offer follows the real model closely: partners feed each other connecting passengers through their hubs, members earn more on routes a partner also flies, and each pays a weekly fee to belong. Codeshares are separate, bilateral deals between two airlines, and both sides must agree.

Some real features have a direct counterpart. Partner feed is the alliance's connecting itinerary: a passenger from your spoke city reaches a partner's long-haul flight with one booking. Every alliance has a private chat board where members can split markets and line up their hubs. In historical worlds, codeshares do not exist until 1990, roughly when the real ones spread.

Others differ on purpose. Headwinds has no joint ventures, so partners never pool revenue, and the alliance and its members are still rivals on the standings, which rank every airline on its own profit. An alliance is limited to eight airlines, so in a busy world no single alliance can take in everyone worth having.

Alliances and gates

In worlds with gate scarcity, airports have a fixed number of gates. There the alliance matters twice. Members together may hold no more than 80% of any airport's gates, which stops a group of friends cornering a hub that no single airline could. In exchange, members can share the spare departure slots on their gates: a partner draws the extra flights it needs from the pool and pays the owner the gate's rent for them plus a markup. Choosing partners

Real alliances pair airlines from different parts of the world. Star Alliance's founders covered North America, Europe and Asia between them, so from the first day they could sell one ticket from almost any North American city to almost any Asian one. The same logic holds in Headwinds. A partner hubbed on another continent multiplies your connecting reach, because its passengers feed your network and yours feed its. A partner hubbed at the airport next door mostly splits the demand you were already competing for.

  • Look at their network before their size. Joining a large alliance is worth little if no partner flies where your passengers want to connect.
  • Count the fee. A small airline in a large alliance pays the same weekly fee for reach it cannot yet use.
  • Choose active players. An alliance run by someone who has stopped logging in does nothing for you. If the founder leaves, leadership passes to the longest-serving member, or the alliance disbands.
  • Use the codeshare for one partner. If you want the feed from a single airline without joining its alliance, a codeshare gets you interline revenue for a smaller fee.
