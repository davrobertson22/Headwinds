Pricing Against People: Route Economics in Headwinds

In a Headwinds world, every fare you publish is a message to a human being who can read it, undercut it, or call your bluff. The demand model underneath is the same rigorous simulation as the solo game — but the moment a real rival enters your market, route economics stops being an optimization problem and becomes a negotiation you conduct through prices. This guide covers how contested routes actually divide their passengers, and how to come out of that division profitable.

The pool is mostly fixed. Your share isn't.

A city pair generates a certain number of people who want to fly it each week — driven by the populations at both ends, how much tourist and business pull each city has, how much each country flies, the distance between them, and the time of year. A city counts once: New York to London is one pool of travellers whether it's flown from JFK, Newark or LaGuardia, and secondary fields reach fewer of them than the main airport does. Fares move the size of the pool — cheap tickets coax more people into the air, up to a point, and dear ones price them out — but on a pair you share, what you're mostly fighting over is the split.

When two airlines fly the same city pair, from any airport in either city, the simulation divides travellers based on what each carrier offers: fares, how often the flights go, the quality of the product (cabins, catering, lounges, the age of the fleet), brand awareness and reputation, local advertising, and how well each airline's airports serve the trip. There's no seniority rule in the split itself, but time still counts. A newly opened route takes around four months to build up its full market, and an airline's brand travels with it onto every pair it enters — a start-up nobody has heard of reaches less than half the market whatever it charges, while a well-known carrier is dangerous from its first week. That cuts both ways: your fortress can be stormed by an established rival, and theirs can be stormed by you.

Monopoly weeks are the exception in a Headwinds world, not the rule. Your route profits and loads stay private, but your economy fare, frequency, weekly seats and aircraft on every route are on show in your rival dossier — price every route knowing other players are reading it.

Know their fares before you set yours

Solo players price against a model. You price against a person — so scout them. The Rivals tab shows you every airline in the world, and clicking one opens their full dossier: fleet, every route with its economy fare, frequency, weekly seats and aircraft, and how they've been trending. Each route you share with them also gets a head-to-head card showing the projected split, straight from the model that books your passengers. Before you publish fares on a contested pair, know what the other carrier charges, what equipment they fly, and whether the route is a core artery of their network or a stray experiment.

That last distinction drives everything. A player defending a trunk route that feeds their hub will match your cuts all the way to the floor, because losing the route unravels their bank structure. A player with one aircraft moonlighting on the pair will fold and redeploy the moment it stops paying. Same fare chart, opposite correct strategies.

The arithmetic of a fare war

Here's the trap in numbers. Suppose a contested pair splits demand roughly evenly and both carriers are modestly profitable. You cut fares 20% to take share. Two things happen: your yield drops on every passenger you were already carrying, and your rival — who watches their dashboard as closely as you watch yours — matches within a week. The split returns to roughly where it was. Cheaper fares do tempt some extra travellers onto the pair, but only into seats someone has spare — if you were both flying fairly full, they have nowhere to sit, and now both of you fly the same passengers for 20% less revenue. The pool doesn't care about your feud.

Fare wars in Headwinds are won by the airline with the lower cost base or the deeper cash reserves, and by nobody else. Before you start one — or decide to fight one that starts — audit exactly those two things. If your rival flies newer, thriftier aircraft on the pair, or their treasury dwarfs yours, the war is lost before the first cut. Factor in their brand, too: a strong reputation and a mature loyalty programme make an airline's passengers less sensitive to price, so they lose fewer travellers to each cut than the fare gap suggests. Concede the route, or compete on something other than price: schedule depth, cabin quality, or the connecting traffic your network funnels through the pair (theirs can't, if their hub is elsewhere).

The strongest deterrent in the game costs nothing: a visible willingness to match. Players who instantly answer any undercut soon find nobody bothers undercutting them.

Yield discipline when a human is watching

The load-factor trap from solo play gets sharper in multiplayer. A route pinned at full is the game telling you there's room in your price — and the better known your brand, the more room there is, because once the aircraft is full a strong brand lets you hold a higher fare without emptying it. Rivals can't see your loads or your route profits, but they can see a fare sitting well under its reference on a big market, and the Route Finder tells them exactly how big that market is. Packed flights at low prices practically invite a competitor to enter above you with a premium product. Keeping fares at the level the market genuinely bears makes your routes look exactly as attractive as they are, and no more.

The same discipline applies to reading your own results. A route's profit falling means one of three different things: the whole world's costs rose (check the fuel index), the market split (check who entered), or your own pricing drifted. The Routes page's Fully-loaded profit view — which charges each aircraft's lease and maintenance to the routes it flies, split by block hours — separates routes that genuinely earn from routes that merely look busy, and it's the number to watch when deciding what survives a war.

The fare cliff

In every world, demand thins as a fare climbs above its reference and runs out entirely at three times it. Worlds created with New World Restrictions — the default for new worlds — add a cliff on top: past a point between 10% and 25% above the reference fare, demand collapses fast, losing roughly 14% for every further 1% on the price. Where the cliff starts depends on the route. A better product earns more headroom, and a rival on the pair brings the economy cliff closer, because competition lowers what travellers think a fair price is. Premium cabins have cliffs of their own, measured against their own reference fares; passengers you price out of them trade down to economy, so the aircraft can stay full while the premium revenue goes.

You don't have to guess where the edge is. The fare editor flags any cabin priced past the cliff and names the exact fare where it starts, and the Routes page separates routes that are past the cliff and actually losing passengers from routes that are past it but still full. Treat the second kind as borrowed time: a rival entering or a quiet month is all it takes to tip them over.

Fuel spikes are shared weather

When fuel spikes in a Headwinds world, it spikes for everyone at once. That changes the correct response compared to solo play: a cost shock all carriers share is the safest moment in the game to raise fares, because every rational rival faces the same pressure to follow. Watch what your competitors do in the first week of a spike — the ones who panic-cut to grab share while their costs are peaking are burning reserves you'll be glad they don't have later. Hedging works the same as in the solo game, but with an extra edge: a hedge bought in calm weeks means a spike hurts your rivals more than it hurts you, and windows where you're strong while others are strained are when territory changes hands.

The clock changes the math

Worlds end. Every Headwinds world runs on a schedule with a final week, and route economics has to respect it. An investment that takes years to pay back is brilliant early in a world and indefensible in its final stretch. As the endgame approaches, shift from planting to harvesting: favour routes that earn now over routes that might mature, stop entering wars you can't win before the buzzer, and remember how the world is scored: the standings rank every airline on its average weekly net profit over the last 52 weeks, and the title goes to the best average over the final 52. A route that only starts paying in the last few months barely moves that average; one that bleeds through the final year drags it down every week it flies.

Where the easy money hides

Every world has crowded arenas and empty ones. The pairs connecting famous cities attract three carriers by mid-game and stay bloody forever. Meanwhile, secondary markets — the mid-sized pair nobody glamorous wants, the regional feed only you have equipment for — sit uncontested with their whole demand pool for the taking. A monopoly on a modest route routinely out-earns a one-third share of a marquee one. Scan for pairs no rival serves — the Route Finder's Rivals column counts carriers flying a market from any airport in either city, which is the only kind of "uncontested" that counts. Being demonstrably hard to compete with in your own niche is worth more than being present in everyone else's.
