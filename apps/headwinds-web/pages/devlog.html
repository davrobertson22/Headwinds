Devlog

What's changing in Headwinds, update by update. Headwinds shares its simulation engine with Tailwinds, so engine improvements — economy rebalances, the hub overhaul, loyalty rework, new aircraft — land here too; those are chronicled on the Tailwinds devlog. This page covers what's new in the multiplayer game itself.

September 3, 2026

Three more propliners — and cruise speed is on the card at last

All from one Discord thread, out of a world started in 1947.

  • Curtiss C-46 Commando. The catalogue’s earliest freighter was the 1959 An-12, which meant cargo simply did not exist in a 1950 world. The C-46 fixes that: war-surplus, 6.8 tonnes, off a 3,600 ft gravel strip, slow and thirsty for what it lifts, and orphaned — Curtiss-Wright left the aircraft business in 1951, so it carries its own parts problem and its own maintenance family.
  • Canadair North Star. A DC-4 airframe with a DC-6 nose and Rolls-Royce Merlins instead of radials: 520 km/h against the DC-4’s 365, 6,200 km against 4,000, and 55 seats against 44. You pay for it in fuel, in an overhaul bill no other type shares, and in a cabin so loud that passengers complained in writing.
  • Lockheed L-1049 Super Constellation. The stretched Connie as first delivered, four years before the 1049G already in the game: plain R-3350s, no tip tanks, 88 seats. In 1951 it is the only alternative to a Stratocruiser above 80 seats — and unlike the G it will not cross an ocean.
  • Cruise speed is a listed specification. It always drove block time — how many rotations a week a frame can fly — but you could only infer it. It is now a stat on every market card, a sortable column in the comparison table, on the checkout header, and in the aircraft guide. A 350 km/h propliner flies a third of the sectors a jet does on the same route, and now the market says so before you buy.

September 3, 2026

The head-to-head card now tells you who actually wins

“Why am I losing this battle?” — asked, fairly, by a player whose contested-route card showed them ahead on price, quality and frequency while their planes flew half-empty. The card was only showing the rows where they were winning.

  • Projected share. Every contested route now shows how the market actually splits between you and your rivals — straight from the same demand model that books your passengers each week, so the card can never disagree with your results.
  • Brand awareness warning. A young airline isn’t considered by most travellers yet, whatever it charges — an unknown brand reaches less than half the market. When low awareness is what’s costing you the fight, the card now says so, instead of leaving you to blame your fares.
  • Business cabin warning. All-economy on a pair where a rival sells business class means the entire business segment books away from you. The card now flags it and tells you the share of the market you’re conceding.

September 2, 2026

Every aircraft is back on the classic market — the old ones arrive old

Reported on the Discord: the DC-4, DC-6, DC-7 and C-47 had vanished from a 2026 world. They had — the 1950s propliners added for era worlds were showing up in present-day worlds as the cheapest seats in the catalogue by a mile (a Vickers Vanguard leased for a fifth of an ATR 72, per seat, with the same maintenance bill), so they had been hidden for a night. Hiding aircraft is the wrong fix.

  • The whole catalogue is on the 2026 market again, propliners included. The only type you can never buy in a classic world is the Comet 1, whose certificate was pulled in 1954.
  • Vintage metal now arrives at a vintage age. Out-of-production types have always been delivered used — sixteen years old at most. That was tuned for 1970s and ’80s jets; for a line closed in the 1950s it made no sense. A type whose line has been closed fifty years or more now arrives twenty years old, climbing a year per year to a ceiling of thirty, and the card says so. At thirty years the maintenance curve bills 5.5× base rather than 2.28×, which is what makes a DC-6 a labour of love rather than a business plan.
  • Vintage types are bought outright. No lessor stocks a sixty-year-old airliner, restricted world or not; the lease button says why. Anything with a younger line — the 737-200, the F27, the L-1011 — leases as before.
  • Frames already in a fleet keep the age they were delivered at. Nothing anyone owns changes.

September 2, 2026

Era worlds: new-metal prices, honest ages, and the C-47

Reported on the Discord after the first 1950 world opened: aircraft that were plainly in production were showing up as used, and the Viscount was on sale three years before it flew.

  • In-production types now arrive new — and say so. The market was quoting every 1950s type at its present-day second-hand age, so a Convair 240 with the line still open read as sixteen years old with the maintenance bill to match. The frames you actually received were always new; the store now says what the hangar delivers. Once a line closes, frames arrive progressively older, and thirty years after the last one rolled out there are none left to buy.
  • “Not yet in service” is on the card. Types listed up to three years ahead of their entry into service — so you can plan the fleet you’ll be buying — now carry a badge saying so on the card and in the table, not just a locked button.
  • New metal costs new-metal money. Every price in the catalogue is a present-day figure, and for a type whose line closed decades ago that is what a used one fetches today. Era worlds were selling a factory-fresh Convair 240 in 1950 at that used price. While a production line is open, a type now sells and leases at two and a half times its catalogue figure, sliding back down over the twelve years after the line closes as the used market fills. Leases already signed keep their rate for the term. The same fix closes a hole where a new-built aircraft was valued far above what you paid for it the week you bought it.
  • The Douglas C-47 Skytrain. Most postwar start-ups didn’t begin with a DC-3; they began with the surplus military version of it. The C-47 joins the catalogue: same airframe with a cargo door and a bare cabin re-seated to 28, cheaper per seat than the DC-3 and rougher to keep running. It’s the cheapest way into the air in a 1950 world.

August 27, 2026

Every hub you build now wears a gold pin on the network map

Reported on the Discord: a map showing four hubs as ordinary spokes.

  • The map was only ever pinning the airport you founded at. Hubs and focus cities you designate later — the ones you bought gates for, paid the construction bill on and now run connecting banks through — drew as plain blue dots like any spoke. Every station you have designated is now gold-pinned, and the pin grows with the tier, so a focus city, a hub, a major hub and an international gateway are told apart at a glance. Hovering one names its tier.
  • A brand-new hub shows up before its first route does. An airport you have designated but not yet flown from was missing from the map entirely; it now appears the moment the designation is live, which is rather the point of looking at the map while you plan. More on what each tier buys you in the hub strategy guide.

August 27, 2026

Multi-stop routes bend on the map — and can finally take more flights and more aircraft

Reported on the Discord: “I fly a multi-leg route, MCI-JFK-ORY. On the map, it shows a line from MCI to ORY” — and “I have several other multi leg routes, and they are all full … you can add more weekly round trips and more aircraft to that route from the routes page, but not for multi leg routes.”

  • The map draws every stop. A rotation like MCI–JFK–ORY used to appear as a single arc from Kansas City to Paris, passing hundreds of kilometres from the airport it actually lands at. The line now bends through each stop in order and is drawn in the same purple the routes page uses for multi-stop. Hovering names the whole chain and its leg count; focusing one frames the entire rotation instead of just its ends.
  • A rotation is its own route on the map. If you fly both MCI–JFK–ORY and a plain MCI–ORY, they used to be merged into one line and one row with their numbers added together. They are two products and now read as two.
  • Filtering the map by an airport finds rotations that stop there, not just the ones that begin or end there.
  • Multi-stop routes have a flights-per-week stepper, the same one single-leg routes have. When it refuses, it names the airport that ran out of slots — including the one in the middle, which is usually the one that’s full.
  • “+ Add Aircraft” puts a second aircraft on a rotation without closing and rebuilding it. The stops are locked, and the fares it starts from are the ones that rotation already sells, so your two tails don’t undercut each other.
  • Freight lanes get the same treatment. Flights and yield were already editable on the routes page; “+ Add Freighter” now opens the freight planner on that lane, so a freighter that has run out of block hours isn’t the end of the lane.
  • Slot arithmetic counts the stops in the middle. A rotation calling at an airport uses two movements there per cycle — an arrival and a departure. The gate-slot checks now count them everywhere, so a busy connecting airport can no longer be quietly oversold by a frequency bump or a new route. Passenger and freight also count against each other now: a freighter occupies a gate exactly like a jet, and the two halves of your airline were previously able to book the same slot twice.
  • Regulations follow the aeroplane. A one-stop used to be a way around a rule: the legs of a rotation were checked, the market it actually sells was not, so a service into a restricted airport could be flown at any frequency by adding a stop. Each leg is now judged on the departures it operates, and the route is judged on the market it sells — and where an airport’s rules give a limited number of exemptions, a nonstop and a one-stop on the same city pair share one, rather than each taking a full allowance.
  • A refused route now tells you which rule refused it, in the regulator’s own words, and on a rotation it names the leg. “Regulatory frequency cap” on a route that is banned outright at any frequency sent people hunting for a cap that was never the problem.

August 27, 2026

Find any airport in the world from the Airports tab

  • A search box at the top of Airports now reaches every airport in the game. The old one only searched the region you had opened, so looking up Honolulu meant knowing it was filed under Oceania first — or going to the route finder or the gate list to see its numbers. Type a code, a city, an airport name or a country and click straight through to the airport page: gates and slots, who else flies there, your presence and its connectivity.

August 27, 2026

Cabin refits work — and now take the aircraft into the shop

Reported on the Discord: “cabin reconfigs dont appear to work … it goes through but the plane isnt taken out of service the next week, nor does it actually change configurations”. Two separate faults met in that one report.

  • Refits weren’t reaching the world at all. Confirming a new cabin closed the screen and did nothing — no change, no charge, no error. The request was being dropped in your browser before it ever left for the server, so a refit you thought you’d booked simply never happened. Fixed: cabin changes now save like every other decision.
  • A refit is real work now. The cost banner has always said the aircraft comes out of service for refitting. It never did. Reconfiguring a cabin now sends the aircraft to the shop: one week for most narrowbodies and regionals, two for widebodies and up, plus a week when you’re moving a quarter of the seats or more. The screen quotes the exact number of weeks before you commit, and any routes the aircraft flies stop earning until it’s back — so re-cabining a fleet mid-season is a decision with a price attached.
  • An aircraft already in the shop can’t take a cabin job. Select a tail that’s in a C or D check, or grounded by a failure, and the refit screen says so and leaves it out of the batch. A batch charges you only for the aircraft it actually refits.
  • Refitting aircraft say “Refitting” on the fleet list and the route tables, not “Grounded — in repair”. Nothing is broken; it’s in the hangar because you sent it there.
  • Maintenance checks now show time until due, not time flown. The check panel used to print hours accrued — the same number under both C and D on a young airframe, which is why it read as total time. It now reads due in ~6w (766h left), estimated from how hard that particular aircraft is actually flying, so a hard-worked jet shows a nearer date than a spare. Thresholds and check costs are unchanged.

August 26, 2026

Your head office now costs what your aeroplanes are worth

This started as an argument on the Discord about whether regional and island-hopper airlines are impossible to run. Rather than trade opinions we went and counted: 144 airlines across six live worlds, 105 accounts. The claim was right, and the reason turned out to be one line in the overhead formula rather than anything about the aircraft.

  • The head office was billed per airframe, not per aeroplane. Overhead counted how many aircraft you had and nothing about what they were, so two turboprops and two A380s were charged exactly the same $68,495 a week. Across the fleet, weekly revenue spans about fifty times from the smallest gauge to the largest; overhead spanned less than two. Airlines that opened on aircraft under 80 seats died at 70%, and 11 of 13 never recorded a single profitable week — against 38% for a narrowbody start. A two-turboprop startup flying one round trip a day grossed $48,734 a week against a $68,495 head office. That is 141% of gross revenue before a litre of fuel.
  • Fixed costs now follow a curve through seat count. Head-office overhead and all four crew groups read a curve anchored at the same medians every calibrated figure in the game was set against, so nothing that was already right has moved. What has gone is the cliff at each category boundary: a 290-seat 767-200ER used to pay 72% more fixed cost than a 295-seat 757-300, which had quietly handed the 757-300 the lowest break-even load factor in the game.
  • The curve now reaches the bottom of the catalogue. It used to stop at 39 seats and clamp, so a nine-seat Islander was charged a 39-seater’s head office and crew, and every type under about 21 seats lost money with every seat sold at the maximum legal frequency. Incapable, not uncompetitive — and it stranded the 25 airports in the game with runways under 4,000 feet, which exist for exactly those aircraft. A Twin Otter on the St Barths shuttle goes from −$4,275 a week to +$12,638, and now wins more missions across the route grid than any other small aircraft.
  • Who this moves, and by how much. Extending the curve to the bottom of the catalogue is a pure reduction — nothing under 39 seats got dearer. Replacing the category steps with a curve cuts both ways: of the airlines we measured, 19 are better off and 26 slightly worse, the largest rise being 1.85% of revenue for a fleet of five double-deckers and the largest fall 6.5% for a fleet of 164 turboprops. Everyone in a world moves at the same moment, so relative standings should hold broadly steady.
  • Small aircraft still ask something of you. The small operators who were already making money were all doing the same thing — very high frequency on very few short routes; one flies 41 weekly frequencies on a single pair. At four times the utilisation, a turboprop’s head office falls from 141% of revenue to 35%. That lever was always there and nothing in the game taught it. It is worth a read of fleet planning before you write off a small gauge.

August 26, 2026

Three screens that were telling you less than they knew

  • The market’s efficiency bar only ever measured fuel. The green bar on every aircraft card scored fuel burn per seat and nothing else — no maintenance, no crew, no cost of owning the thing — under a heading that read like the total cost of a seat. That is how a 767-300ER could sit a point above a 737-800 while billing three times the maintenance. The bar now says Fuel per seat, and cards and the comparison table carry a Maint/seat figure beside the weekly bill — Maint/tonne on freighters, which never had a comparison number at all. Nothing about what aircraft cost has changed; the screen stopped hiding half the answer.
  • The route finder credits connecting traffic, and the lease rate you actually signed. Its profit column deducted the catalogue lease rate from every candidate — including aircraft you own outright, which pay no rent at all, and leased tails whose real rate is whatever they signed at. It also ignored the connecting feed a hub generates, which the weekly tick has always credited to the route. Both are in the column now, so a lane off your hub reads as the tick will settle it.
  • The watermark across the world map. Reported on the Discord at 6:43 in the morning, which is dedication. Our map tiles come from a third party who changed their terms overnight and began painting “API key required” diagonally across every tile — served as a perfectly valid image, so nothing failed and nothing logged; the only signal was a player’s screenshot. The map now requests tiles with a key.

August 25, 2026

Crew are hired and trained now, not conjured

Labour has been a wage bill with a pay dial on it. In new worlds it is a workforce: people you recruit, pay to train, wait for, and lose if you underpay them. This is the largest change to the operating side of the game in a while, so it goes to new worlds only — switching it on under a live season would strand every airline in a world at the same moment, which is not a thing to do to people mid-season.

  • Hiring pays for training up front and delivers later. Each of the four groups has its own lead time and its own bill per crew unit: pilots ten weeks at $45,000 (a type rating and line training), maintenance six weeks at $18,000, cabin crew five weeks at $12,000, ground staff two weeks at $4,000. Ordering aircraft and ordering the people to fly them are now two different lead times you have to line up.
  • Pay is a retention decision, not just a cost. Every week a fraction of your people leave, at a rate set by what you pay and how they feel about working for you. Cutting pay to survive a bad quarter now costs you the crew as well as the morale.
  • Short-handed degrades long before it grounds. Under 15% short, the operation simply gets worse — on-time performance slips and satisfaction follows it down. At or past 15% on the flight-crew groups, aircraft you have nobody to fly stand down for the week. The crew-hungriest tails park first, so the largest possible number of aircraft keep flying, and a parked tail flies again the moment its crew arrive; nothing is written off.
  • Your first two aircraft crew instantly. Headwinds already delivers a new airline’s first two aircraft immediately, so a new competitor can start flying rather than idle for real hours waiting on metal. A ten-week pilot queue would have undone that, so the starter allowance staffs those two aircraft from the day you join.
  • Anything already flying is fully staffed. Where the system does apply to an existing airline, it starts fully crewed with nobody in training — you are never handed a staffing crisis you did not create.
  • Operations shows the whole picture. Per group: crewed against required, how far short you are, who is in training and how many weeks until they are on the line, and hire buttons that quote the bill and the wait before you commit.

August 25, 2026

New worlds now start with the restrictions on

New World Restrictions have been an opt-in experiment since late July. They are now the default for a newly created world, and classic rules are an explicit opt-out on the create form. Existing worlds are untouched, and always will be.

  • Lessors carry single-deck, previous-generation aircraft only. No double-deckers at any age — which matters, because the cheap answer to a ban on the A380 was a 747-400 at 71% of the A380’s seats for a fraction of its rent. The largest thing you can lease is a 777-200ER or A330-300 at 440 seats: 2.33 times a 737-800’s seats per slot, down from 4.51. Buying is untouched, because capital already gates it.
  • The lease order book is capped at five aircraft, or a quarter of your active fleet once you are past twenty. A rate limit on how fast a fleet can appear, not a ceiling on how large it can get.
  • Head office is charged per departure, by aircraft class — $100 for a turboprop up to $7,500 for a double-decker — rather than by the size of your fleet. Only flights that actually operate are billed, so a dormant seasonal route costs nothing.
  • The reference fare ladder sits 5% lower, passenger and cargo alike. It shipped at 15% and that was far too deep — the work of squeezing margins belongs in the cost model, not in a blanket cut to what everyone can charge.

These sit alongside the labour and cost work of the last month, which together bring a comfortable operation’s margin from about 30% to about 15% — a good deal closer to a real airline. If you would rather have the classic game, the toggle is right there when you create the world.

August 25, 2026

The 737 family, priced like a family

A catalogue pass over the most-flown aircraft in the game. Every figure here is a correction to something that was wrong, and the effects reach aircraft you may already own.

  • The MAX 8 was carrying the MAX 8-200’s seat count. 210 is the -200’s certified maximum, not the MAX 8’s — which made the -200 dead weight in the catalogue: worse per seat for a discount that never paid back. The MAX 8 is now 189 seats, the 737-800 fuselage it shares; the -200 is 202 and now costs more than the MAX 8, which is the right way round.
  • The MAX 7 burns what a stretch of the -700 burns. It had been given the -700’s burn with the re-engine factor applied, which left it beating the MAX 8 per seat — a smaller aircraft undercutting a larger one on unit cost, which does not happen.
  • The 737-900ER arrives at the age it should. It was the only NG banded off its own 2007 service entry rather than the production line it was built on, so it turned up on the used market older than it is.
  • Classics are a real cheap-entry tier now. The -300 at $9.5M, the -500 at $10.5M and the -200 Advanced at $6M — the -200 had somehow become dearer than a strictly better -300. The -400’s burn and maintenance are corrected, and the 737-300F’s burn was 15% above published block fuel, which had the 18-tonne freighter out-burning the 20-tonne one.
  • Store cards quote maintenance as delivered, not factory-fresh. A ten-year-old Classic bills about 1.5 times the base rate from its first week with you. The card showed you the base rate and let you find out later.

August 25, 2026

Bankrupt airlines stop crowding the world

Asked for on the Discord, and fair: a world that has run for a season carries more dead airlines than live ones, and every one of them was still taking up a seat and a row.

  • Standings show the airlines still flying. The fallen are counted rather than listed — “N airlines have gone bankrupt or bowed out here” — so the table is the competition, not the graveyard.
  • A dead airline no longer counts against the world’s limit. The player cap bounds how many airlines fly, not how many have ever existed, so a world that looked full to a newcomer now has the seats it should. Someone re-founding is still let back in regardless.
  • Nothing is deleted. Your row survives, which is what lets you re-found in place, keeps the anti-rejoin guard honest, and lets the season record be banked at the end — a bankrupt season still counts, and the comeback awards depend on it. What is cleared is the week-by-week detail of an airline that will never tick again: 86 dead airlines against 65 live ones were being re-read on every standings poll, which is a fair chunk of why the lobby felt slow.
  • The fire sale is unchanged — a failed airline’s fleet still goes to the used market at a distressed price, and its gates are still listed where gates are scarce.

August 25, 2026

Three from the Discord, and an error message you can actually read

  • A freighter’s route breakdown said the aircraft was idle. The aircraft detail panel read passenger routes only, so a 767F with ten lanes showed the empty “idle” state directly under a utilisation box reading “10 routes”. Freight lanes now appear where they should, priced in tonnes a week and dollars per tonne-kilometre rather than passengers and ticket price.
  • Puerto Rico is US soil. San Juan was being read as a foreign country for hub upgrades, the foreign focus-city cap, international destination counts and even tail numbers — a US carrier’s aircraft registered under an invented prefix. There is now a proper table of territories that fly under a mainland registry: US, French, Danish and Dutch territories and the British crown dependencies. Deliberately unchanged for now: whether San Juan to Miami is a domestic demand pair, which is a balance decision rather than a bug.
  • “No cover — 1 route idle” with a spare aircraft sitting at the origin. Both halves of that badge were wrong about how a week runs. Breakdowns are rolled at the end of a tick, so a tail that has just gone tech cannot be covered yet; and the downtime countdown runs before the reserve pass, so a tail with one week left takes its own routes back and correctly needs no cover at all. The badge now runs the real pass against your projected fleet and tells you which it is: “back in service next week”, “cover starts next week” with the reserve named, or the actual reason there is none.
  • An error page is not an error message. At about one o’clock Pacific on the 25th, signing in broke for a few hours: our authentication provider’s edge answered with a “connection timed out” page instead of a response. That happens. What should not have happened is what our client did with it — it took the entire HTML page and printed it into the interface, so the sign-in button rendered a couple of hundred lines of raw markup at you. The game itself stayed up and anyone already signed in kept playing; everything was back by the evening. Every player-facing error now collapses markup and walls of text into one readable line.

August 24, 2026

The black screen, and why it happened

On Monday morning a deploy replaced every world screen with a black page — every player, every device, every browser. It lasted most of the afternoon. This is what it was.

  • It was not the servers. The API was on the current build, all six worlds were ticking within a week of each other, and every endpoint answered normally throughout. Reports of “the servers are down” were entirely reasonable and entirely wrong.
  • It was one line of ours, in your browser. A new annotation on the join form — the count of airlines already based at an airport — was added below the point where the screen returns “loading…”. On the first render the world data has not arrived and that line never runs; on the second it does, and React refuses a render that does more work than the one before it. It refuses by tearing down the whole tree, and what is left is the dark background.
  • That is why it “worked fine” for some people. Anyone with a tab already open was still running the previous build and noticed nothing — and anyone who cleared their cache to fix it made their own situation strictly worse. Sorry to whoever spent an hour on that.
  • We had a check for exactly this, and it was skipping most of the file. The tool that scans for this mistake stripped string literals line by line, and treated the apostrophe in the words “Auth isn’t configured” as the start of a string — blanking eleven hundred lines of the file, including the screen that broke. It has been rewritten to parse the code properly rather than read it as text, and it carries a regression test with that apostrophe in it.

August 24, 2026

A season ends with a ceremony now

A world reached its final week, the standings were computed, and then they were thrown away. There has been a career record waiting for them since launch. Seasons now finish properly.

  • The season is scored and published when the world ends. A champion, a podium, and awards: Iron Throne for the most weeks spent at number one, Busiest for passengers carried, Biggest Network for routes flown, and Best Comeback for the highest finish by an airline that re-founded after going under. An award nobody earned is not given out.
  • An end-of-season screen. Champion, podium, the awards, where you finished out of how many, and the full final standings underneath. It opens once when you arrive in a concluded world, and a Results button in the topbar reopens it whenever you like.
  • Concluded seasons have a hall of fame on the worlds screen, newest first, each with its champion.
  • Every in-game year now gets a year in review in the news feed: who leads, the podium, and the biggest climber of the year. A game year is fifty-two weeks and used to pass without a word.
  • An airline that never went public is unranked, not last. Staying private is a decision; recording it as twentieth of twenty would misreport it.

August 24, 2026

Your name, your profile, and messages that follow you between worlds

Until now you existed only inside a world. An airline was a row in a table and the person flying it was a display name with nothing behind it.

  • Every player has a public profile. Click a name in the standings, the news feed, a rival dossier, an alliance roster or a message and you get the account behind it: the airlines they are flying now, the seasons they have finished, and the badges they have earned. Private worlds stay private — a finish in an invite-only world reads as “Played” unless it was a podium, and the totals are recomputed from the public subset rather than quietly leaking the rest.
  • Usernames. Claim one for free; after that one rename every thirty days, with an audit trail so an old name stays traceable rather than being a way to walk away from a reputation. Your username is your display name everywhere it exists.
  • Direct messages at the account level. An inbox in the lobby and beside the feed in-world, so a message reaches you whichever world you are in — or none. Who may write to you is your choice: everyone, people you share a world with (the default, and finished seasons count), or nobody. Blocks and reports work without a world attached, and a refusal reads identically whether it is your policy or a block, so nobody learns they were personally blocked.
  • The message button only appears when a message will go through, rather than letting you write one and refusing it afterwards.
  • The standings gained a column and a marker. Share value per share replaces market cap in the table (market cap is now a tooltip), and a rival who has not made a move in three real days is marked idle — useful when you are deciding whether the airline squatting on the gates you want is still being flown.
  • Re-founding makes the news. “X is back — re-founded”, with the hub. A returning airline reuses its row, so the joined item could never report it.
  • The join form tells you what you are walking into — how many airlines are already based at the airport you are typing, before you commit to it.

August 24, 2026

Cargo is contested, and a sweep through the data

The freight half of the game had a strategic hole in it, and the map underneath had a few things wrong. Most of this is shared engine work — the Tailwinds devlog has the fleet detail — but the first item matters more here than anywhere.

  • Rival freighters now compete for your tonnage. Cargo demand on a lane was split among your own routes and nobody else’s: a competitor could run freighters on the same lane and take nothing off you. In a world full of other airlines that made an uncontested cargo network the strongest thing you could build, which is not a market, it is a subsidy. Contested lanes now split on capacity and yield like passenger routes do. Lanes only you serve are unchanged.
  • The A380 is priced like the flagship it is, three widebodies that were strictly worse than a neighbour got corrections, and double-deckers and the supersonic now pay for the three-pilot flight deck they lay over with, rather than being charged for fewer crew than an ordinary widebody.
  • Two of New York’s outlying airports were carrying the whole metro’s population. Westchester and Stewart were each priced as if twenty million people lived on their doorstep, which made them look like trunk airports. They are now sized for the districts they actually serve. Kennedy, Newark and LaGuardia are unchanged.
  • City pairs a train does better no longer sell air tickets. Hong Kong to Shenzhen is 38 kilometres; Singapore to Johor Bahru, Amsterdam to Rotterdam and Baltimore to Washington are all under 50. Nobody books those. Only the direct hop is suppressed — both cities keep every other route they fly — and short hops with water in the way are explicitly spared, because Nantucket to Hyannis and Saba to St Maarten are real flights with no road alternative.

A career that outlives the world, and codeshares that both airlines agreed to

Two days of work across a twenty-eight item audit. Most of it was engine and economy — fuel hedging, crew costs by aircraft size, union grievances, freight seasons, hub connectivity, real load factors, and a fair bit more — and because that engine is shared, it is written up on the Tailwinds devlog. It all landed here too, and some of it moves your numbers noticeably. This entry covers what changed in the multiplayer game itself.

Your record now survives the world it was set in. A season ended, the standings were computed, and then they were thrown away — they existed only inside the transaction that wrote the final week. There was a column in the database waiting for a career since the day the game launched, and nothing had ever written to it.

  • Every finished season is recorded against your account: the world, the airline you flew, where you finished, how many weeks you were in it, and your lifetime passengers carried.
  • Badges are earned from that record — champion, podium, veteran, a million passengers, and phoenix for finishing a season you went bankrupt in. They are worked out from your history every time they are shown rather than stored, so the rules can improve without anybody’s past being rewritten.
  • An airline that never went public is unranked, not last. Staying private is a decision, and recording it as “20th of 20” would misreport it. A bankrupt airline still gets its season on the record — you were there.
  • Worlds that ended before this shipped are backfilled from their final standings. Lifetime passengers for those seasons read as unknown rather than zero, because the honest answer is that the figure is years stale.

Your career panel appears under the worlds screen once you have a finished season. Before that it shows nothing at all — a hall of fame full of zeroes is worse than none.

A codeshare is a deal now, not something you do to someone. Signing one against a human rival took a fee off you and paid you interline revenue computed from a real player’s network — while that player was never asked and never told. It was the only bilateral agreement in the game that was not, in fact, bilateral.

  • You offer, they accept. Offers appear on both airlines’ Alliances tab. Accepting writes the agreement to both sides in one transaction — if either side’s engine refuses it, the whole thing is refused, because half a bilateral agreement is worse than none.
  • Offering someone who has already offered you signs it immediately. Both of you plainly consented; there is no reason to make you wait.
  • Cancelling ends both halves. Ending only your side would leave the other player paying a weekly fee for a partner who had stopped carrying their passengers.
  • Unanswered offers expire after eight weeks — not just tidiness: a dead offer sitting between two airlines would have blocked them from ever offering again.

An airline that fails now leaves something behind for everyone else. Bankruptcy was a status change and a single news item. The fleet froze inside a save nobody would read again, the orderbook stopped existing, and the gates went back to the pool in silence — and only on gate-scarcity worlds, because that was the one branch anybody had written. A carrier could go under with forty aircraft and a dozen slots at a fortress hub and leave the world exactly as it found it.

  • The fleet goes to the used market at a distressed price. An administrator sells into a market that knows it has to, and the listing names the airline that failed.
  • The gates are listed as administrator’s sales on worlds where gates are scarce, priced off each airport’s own auction reserve — a Heathrow slot is not valued like a regional one.
  • Nobody gets paid. The money leaves the world rather than being credited to a company that no longer exists. Every check that protects you as the buyer still applies.

A rival going under should be an opportunity for everyone still flying, and now it is.

The interest rate on your loans is no longer the client’s to choose. A modified client could ask for the 3% floor on a loan that should have cost 18%, and the server would write it down as asked — about $790,000 a year of interest that simply did not happen. Credit scoring now lives in the shared engine, computed from what your airline actually earned rather than from a forecast the server never had, and the server prices every loan itself.

Batched fleet actions are safe in multiplayer. Selling, retiring, scheduling checks or extending leases across a selection runs inside one write, with the number of aircraft capped so a huge list cannot hold row locks while everyone else waits. If one aircraft cannot be actioned it is skipped and reported rather than aborting the batch — seven of twelve checks scheduled before the money ran out is the honest outcome.

July 27, 2026

Capital markets: your share price is the scoreboard now

Read this one before you next open your airline, because your numbers are going to move. Valuations across every live world have been rebased, and most airlines will see their market cap drop by roughly half on the next tick. Nothing has gone wrong and you haven’t lost anything — the old valuation was simply wrong, in a way that made the stock market a money printer.

Here is what was broken. An airline’s value was built on its profit multiplied by up to 32, which meant every extra $100k a week of profit added something like $166M of market cap. One good route was worth a quarter of a billion dollars. Prices then climbed toward that number at a capped 20% a week — smoothly, predictably, for weeks on end. A stock that reliably returns 20% a week against a 3% round trip isn’t an investment, it’s a vending machine.

Airlines are now valued the way real airlines are: the lowest-multiple, most cyclical business there is, on a P/E of roughly 5 to 13, with the earnings component capped against actual revenue so no valuation can outrun the business underneath it. Prices move at up to 8% a week instead of 20%.

The market moves, not just your airline

Every world now has a market index: one shared sentiment number that lifts or depresses every airline’s valuation together, drifting through bull and bear phases, and levered to the fuel price. When fuel spikes, the whole sector re-rates down. A rising share price can now mean the market rose rather than that you did well — and a genuinely good quarter can be swamped by a bad market. Timing matters.

You’re ranked on value per share

This is the real change, and it is the reason for everything else. The world standings used to rank on market cap. Market cap measures how big you got, and that quietly made the game reward the wrong things: hoarding cash raised your score, and returning a single dollar to your shareholders lowered it. Every dividend, every buyback, was a self-inflicted wound on the leaderboard. No sane player would ever pay one.

Standings now rank on shareholder value per share — your share price, plus every dividend you have ever paid. It measures what you created for each unit of ownership rather than how much empire you accumulated. Growing a big, profitable airline is still the dominant way to win. What changed is that capital allocation is no longer free.

Your rank will not jump the day this lands: every airline is on the same share count at the moment of the change, so the new ordering starts out identical to the old one. It only begins to diverge as players start issuing and retiring shares.

Your company, on the Stocks tab

There’s a new Your company panel, and four decisions in it that didn’t exist before.

  • Go public. New airlines are now incorporated private: no tradable shares, nobody on your register but you, and no place in the standings. From week 26, with a trading record behind you, you can list — selling 10–35% of the company to investors and putting the cash in your treasury. IPOs price at a discount, and the discount is smaller the longer and more profitably you have traded. Staying private is a legitimate way to play. It just isn’t a way to win.
  • Share offerings. Raise more later, up to 15% of your shares a game year. The discount widens each time you go back to the market, and narrows if you have a record of returning capital — being good to your shareholders buys you cheaper money later.
  • Buybacks. Retire stock from the float. Cash leaves, but the share count falls faster, so it lifts per-share value when your stock is cheap or your cash is sitting idle.
  • Dividends. A standing policy, up to 60% of trailing-quarter profit, paid every 13 weeks. Only shares held outside your founder block get paid, so a dividend costs you in exact proportion to how much of yourself you have sold. Miss a quarter and it suspends automatically — publicly.

One more thing worth knowing: idle cash is now credited at a fraction of face value. The market does not reward a lazy balance sheet. Once you have run out of gates to take and aircraft to order, a pile of unspent cash actively drags on your valuation, and returning it beats sitting on it. That is precisely the point at which real airlines start paying dividends.

There is a real person on the other side of your trade

Trades used to be filled by nobody. Selling shares conjured cash into the world out of thin air, which is why late-game worlds turned into a glut of money. Every world now has a finite float pool of outside-investor cash and shares. Your buys pay into it, your sells draw out of it, and IPOs and offerings are funded from it. As it runs down, sellers get worse fills; if it empties, the equity window shuts and you cannot raise capital at all, because there is no money on the other side. It heals slowly. First mover to the market gets the cheap capital.

Trading is also properly frictional now. A large order moves the price against itself in proportion to how much of the free float it represents, so a big stake can no longer be accumulated at the marked price, and realized gains are taxed at 25%. Reading a rival’s numbers and backing your judgement still pays. It just isn’t free money any more.

If the re-rating looks wrong on your airline, or the new standings read oddly to you, say so in Discord. This is the largest change to how Headwinds is scored since launch and I would rather hear about it early.

One world, one calendar

Players kept reporting that they and their rivals were seeing different dates in the same world, and they were right. This one is worth explaining, because it was doing more damage than a wrong number in the top bar.

A new airline was always created at Year 1, Week 1, no matter what the world clock said. Join a world sitting at Year 3, Week 12 and you didn’t join it in the spring of year three — you started your own private January and ran it alongside everyone else’s calendar for the rest of the game. Because the simulation reads the season off your week, that wasn’t cosmetic: you were flying a different season to your rivals in the same market, catching the summer peak on transatlantic routes while the carrier competing with you was in the depths of February. Anything scheduled by date — aircraft deliveries, fuel hedges, the quarterly rhythm — was keyed to your calendar rather than the world’s.

  • You now join in the world’s here-and-now. A late joiner lands on the current world date, in the current season, on the same week as everyone else.
  • Existing worlds have been corrected. Airlines already running on a private calendar were rebased onto the world clock, with anything scheduled ahead of them shifted to match, so nothing pending was lost in the move.
  • “Joined at” means something now. Your join date used to record the week of the year and throw the year away — someone joining in year three was filed as having joined at week 12. It records the real point in the world’s history.

And the December Week 4 freeze

The same investigation turned up the cause of a bug several of you had reported and none of us could pin down: the game appearing to stop dead at December, Week 4, with ticks landing on the server but nothing moving on screen until you reloaded the page.

The client decides whether to accept a new week by checking that it’s later than the one on screen — and it was comparing the week of the year. Every New Year, week 52 became week 1, which reads as time running backwards, so the client rejected the new week as stale and sat there. Heavy maintenance: your fleet now visits the hangar

Aircraft don't fly forever without care. Headwinds now models the real airline maintenance calendar: your planes build up wear as they fly, and periodically they have to come out of service for a heavy check — the multi-week hangar visits every real carrier plans its fleet around. The light A- and B-checks stay folded into your weekly maintenance budget; the two big ones, the C check and the D check, are now yours to schedule.

  • Wear comes from flying, not just the calendar. Every block-hour an aircraft flies brings its next check closer. Work a jet hard nine times a day and it comes due in months; keep it as a spare and it ages on the calendar instead. A C check lands roughly every two years, a D check every six — and a D covers the C.
  • Checks take planes out of service. A C check is a week or two in the hangar; a D check is a month or more on a widebody, and it isn't cheap — cost scales with the aircraft's value. Time it for a demand trough with a spare ready to cover the route and it barely registers. Get caught in the summer peak with no cover and it hurts.
  • Schedule it your way. Open any aircraft and start a check now, or book one for a future week when the timing suits your network. The Fleet list flags every airframe — due soon, due, overdue, or in the shop.
  • Miss the deadline and the regulator acts. Blow past the grace window and your aircraft is grounded on the spot: longer than a planned check, half again as expensive, and a hit to your reputation. Fleet planning is now a real job.
  • Fresh checks pay you back. A checked airframe breaks down far less often, a D check knocks years off its running costs, and a well-kept plane holds its resale value — while one that's overdue sells at a discount, because nobody overpays to inherit someone else's deferred maintenance.
  • Your crew and hubs help. A happy maintenance team and an in-house base at one of your hubs both bring check costs down — the same standardisation that already rewards a focused fleet.

It's the call every real fleet planner faces: pour money into a 22-year-old airframe's D check, or sell it and move on. Now it's yours to make.

The world's routes, resized to reality

The demand model that decides how many passengers want to fly between any two cities has been recalibrated against real-world airline traffic data. The short version: the game's biggest markets are now the same ones real airlines fight over — and in a game where your rivals are other people, knowing which corridors are truly huge just became a real skill edge.

  • New giants on the map. Routes where flying is the only way — Seoul–Jeju, Tokyo–Sapporo, Sydney–Melbourne, Jeddah–Riyadh — now carry demand worthy of their real-world rank. If nobody in your world is flying them yet, that's an opening.
  • Borders, wealth and tourism now count. Domestic trunks outdraw same-sized international pairs, strong ties (UK–Ireland, Australia–New Zealand, Gulf corridors) keep cross-border demand high, tourist magnets like Cancún, Bali and Orlando draw on their visitors — and long thin routes between weakly connected cities are as marginal as they are in real life.
  • Bigger markets overall. Popular routes carry roughly 1.5–2x the passengers they used to, so expect stronger demand across your network and more room to add frequency — and expect your rivals to notice too.
  • Applies to everyone at once. Your world picks up the new demand at its next weekly tick, hitting every airline in the same moment — nobody gets a head start.

The full breakdown of what changed and why is on the Tailwinds devlog, where engine changes are chronicled.

July 14, 2026

Price every seat, right from the start

Fares used to be a two-step dance: set an economy price to open the route, then dig into the route list afterwards to price the premium cabins. No more — pricing is now one job, done up front.

  • Every cabin, priced before you fly. Opening a route now shows a fare for each cabin your aircraft actually carries — first, business, premium economy and economy — pre-filled at the market rate and ready to adjust before the first departure.
  • One fare editor everywhere. The same editor appears in the route planner, the add-flights form and the route list, with the market reference, your position against it, and a one-click reset. On the Routes tab, look for the Edit button in the Fares column.
  • Honest benchmarks. The reference fares shown for premium cabins were overstated relative to what passengers actually compare against — business and first looked further below market than they were. The benchmarks now match the demand model exactly, so a fare 10% over reference really is 10% over.
  • Forecasts you can trust. The planner's revenue and load projections now use the exact fares you've set, not assumed defaults — what you see before launch is what the route earns after it.

July 14, 2026

Scheduling without the guesswork

A round of quality-of-life work on putting aircraft where you want them.

  • Part-time aircraft can take new work. Route planners now offer any aircraft with spare weekly hours — not just idle ones — so a plane flying a short hop can pick up a second route without you juggling assignments.
  • Change frequency in place. Every route row has a −/+ stepper to add or drop weekly flights on the spot, and when an increase isn't possible it tells you exactly why — gate slots, block hours, or a regulatory cap.
  • Freight explains its ceiling. Long-haul freighters hit their weekly block-hour budget at roughly one rotation a day. Instead of a slider that silently stops, the cargo planner now says so — and points you at the real fix: add another freighter.

A dashboard that tells you why

The home dashboard has been rebuilt from a wall of numbers into a read on how your week actually went — and where the money went with it.

  • A weekly profit bridge. Last week's result sits next to what's projected for the week ahead, with the big movers — revenue, fuel, overhead — broken out so you can see exactly what changed.
  • Know which routes carry you. New True Profit and Last Week views sort your network by what each route really contributes, so the flights holding up the airline — and the ones dragging it down — are obvious at a glance.
  • The numbers that matter, with the story behind them. Load factor, yield, passenger satisfaction and fleet age each show a trend against last week, and every one opens a drill-down so you can chase a figure to its source.

July 12, 2026

Cabins, re-priced

Fitting out your aircraft is clearer now, and the trade-offs are easier to reason about.

  • One less dial. The separate service-quality setting has been retired from aircraft and route setup — it wasn't a lever worth micromanaging plane by plane.
  • Seat quality, honestly priced. Standard economy seats are free, and new aircraft ship that way. Go above it and you'll pay a one-time cost to fit the cabin plus an ongoing cost to keep that standard — so a nicer seat is a real commitment, not a free upgrade.
  • Premium cabins cost to install. Adding premium economy, business, or first now carries a per-seat fitting fee — from $200 a seat up to $1,000 in first — reflecting the real cost of putting those seats in the aircraft.

Smoother worlds, truer numbers

Your airline, charted over time

Player-founded alliances

Alliances in Headwinds aren't the scripted blocs from the solo game — there are no scripted anything in Headwinds. They're founded, grown, and run by players.

  • Found one from the world lobby. Name it and you're the founder. Other airlines request to join; you accept or reject them. Up to 8 active members.
  • Real benefits. Members feed each other connecting traffic, get a demand boost on routes where a partner also flies, and pay a weekly fee that keeps membership a real decision rather than a checkbox.
  • Real governance. If the founder walks away, leadership passes to the longest-serving member — or the alliance disbands. Choose your allies like it matters.

Humans are the competition

The biggest design decision in Headwinds, made permanent: there are no AI airlines. Every competitor on the map is a real person running a real network in your world.

  • Contested routes are player-vs-player. When two airlines fly the same city pair, the demand model splits passengers between them based on fares, frequency, quality, and loyalty — the same economics as always, but the other schedule belongs to a human who will react.
  • The Rivals tab. In multiplayer, the Competition tab becomes Rivals and shows the actual players in your world. The world standings do too — click any airline to open its profile: route network, fleet, alliance, and its recent moves, week by week.
  • No scripted drama. AI fare wars, scripted bankruptcies and startup carriers are all gone. If a fare war breaks out, it's because someone chose to start one.
