Devlog

What's changing in Headwinds, update by update. Headwinds shares its simulation engine with Tailwinds, so engine improvements — economy rebalances, the hub overhaul, loyalty rework, new aircraft — land here too; those are chronicled on the Tailwinds devlog. This page covers what's new in the multiplayer game itself.

November 11, 2026

More speed for big airlines: Routes, Competition, Finance, the map and every click

The third round of speed work, again aimed at the largest airlines. The figures below are from the biggest airline in Scarce Assets, about 4,900 aircraft and 4,000 routes. Smaller airlines gain less because they had less to lose.

  • Routes opens in about a tenth of a second instead of half a second, and a fare change made on it no longer freezes the page for half a second.
  • Competition froze for 1.7 seconds when opened. It now opens in 0.1 to 0.3. With more than 30 contested routes, only the cards on screen are built, so your browser’s find-in-page only sees those.
  • Finance. The profit and loss statement had a row for every route under Catering and Crew Accommodation and for every aircraft under Leases and Maintenance. Those lists now show their first 25 rows with a “Show all” link. The totals are unchanged, and the statement opens in a fifth of a second instead of a full one.
  • The map. The All Routes table under the map now builds only the rows on screen. A fare change with the map open went from 1.5 seconds to 0.6, and a new week from 2.7 seconds to 0.7.
  • Opening and closing routes. Opening a route used to freeze the page twice, about 1.6 seconds each time. It now freezes once, for under a second. Closing one went from about 2 seconds to 1.
  • Every click sends less. After each change the game used to send your whole airline back to your browser, up to 22 MB before compression. It now sends only what the change altered, usually a few hundred bytes.
  • Busy worlds stall less. Once a minute the server re-read every airline in a world to work out what rivals see, which held up every player for a second or more in the largest worlds. It now re-reads only the airlines that changed.

None of the game’s numbers changed.

November 11, 2026

Staffing that runs while you are away, and reserve crews worth paying for

In worlds where airlines hire their own crew, the crew page has been rebuilt and spare staff now have a price and a purpose. This is live in every such world, including ones already running.

  • Keep me fully staffed. One switch on the new Staffing card under Operations. With it on, your leavers are replaced before they are missed and the crews for aircraft on order are hired in time for delivery. It runs on the world's clock, so it keeps working through the weeks you are not logged in. It is on for airlines that join from today; if your airline already exists, tick it once.
  • Everything on one card. Pilots, cabin crew, ground staff and maintenance each get a row: on the line, needed, short or spare, in training, and reserve pay. The old per-group detail is still there behind Show details, and Hire everything needed covers all four groups in one click if you would sooner hire by hand.
  • Reserve staff are no longer free. Trained people above what your fleet needs are paid half a normal wage from this week. If your airline is carrying a surplus, typically after buying a rival and selling its aircraft, you will get one note with the weekly amount. You can keep them, or release those above your reserve level for four weeks' pay each. Tick Release staff above my reserve level and that happens at the end of every week, on the world's clock: aircraft on order keep their crews and staff in training are never released.
  • What a reserve buys. A 10% reserve lifts that group's morale by up to 4 points, adds up to 2 points of on-time performance across pilots, ground staff and cabin crew, and cuts meltdown risk. It also means a newly delivered or leased aircraft flies at once, while an airline with no reserve waits up to ten weeks for pilots. On a route two airlines both want, that wait can decide who gets there first.
  • A reserve level per group. Set none, 2%, 5%, 10%, 15% or 20% for each of the four groups. Keep me fully staffed hires up to the level you choose. Beyond 10% the extra only buys room to grow.
  • Crew on the order form. Ordering or leasing now shows the crew the aircraft need, the training bill, and how many your reserves already cover.
  • Wage figures corrected. The crew cards and the Finance page size each group's bill on aircraft size and, in restricted worlds, on your airline's seniority scale, as the weekly accounts always have.

The reserve numbers are a first setting and may be tuned once a few worlds have run with them.

November 11, 2026

Custom catering in new worlds: your menu, your prices

Worlds created from today can run custom catering. It is a switch on the create-world form, on by default, and the world card shows a Custom catering badge. In those worlds an airline is no longer limited to the five fixed catering levels. Worlds that already exist keep the classic levels and nothing in them changes.

  • Service plans. Under Operations, in the catering section, you build a menu for each cabin from ten items: water, soft drinks, beer and wine, spirits, champagne, a snack, a cold meal, a hot meal, extra courses, and fine dining in business and first. Meals, snacks, wine and champagne come as Basic, Standard or Premium. Basic costs 60% of Standard and passengers value it at 70%; Premium costs 150% and is valued at 125%.
  • Free, or sold at a price you type. A free item goes to every passenger and you pay for every passenger. A sold item goes only to those who buy it. Nothing on the screen tells you what to charge. For each item you see what it costs you per passenger, how many buy at your price, the sales and the net, and a bar for the quality it adds, marked where it would reach if the item were free.
  • It counts against the other airlines on your routes. Catering is part of the quality passengers weigh when they choose between carriers on a shared route. An airline giving away a hot meal and one selling sandwiches are now different products, and the difference shows up in both airlines' costs.
  • One plan does not fit every route. Under 1,000 km a meal counts for half what it does on long haul, and drinks and a snack count for more. From 4,000 km the meal is most of what passengers judge. Costs rise with distance as before.
  • Put a plan on many routes at once. Apply to routes lists your network by flight length, region or hub and shows the change in catering money and quality for each route before you confirm. Plans also appear in every catering picker and can be your default for new routes. Change a plan and every route on it follows from the next week.
  • Catering contracts. A caterer does not cap a plan by level. It leaves out what its kitchen cannot make at the airports it cooks for, you pay only for what is served, and the route screen says what is missing and where.

The classic levels are still on every picker, and Customise on any of them opens a copy to edit. The numbers are a first cut, so say on Discord what feels too cheap, too dear or not worth serving.

November 11, 2026

Thirteen aircraft that were missing from the market

Dunno23 went through the catalogue on Discord and listed the variants it skipped: mostly the originals that the ER models replaced, plus a few rare ones. All thirteen are now in every world, live ones included. In an era world each appears in the year it entered service.

  • The non-ER originals. Boeing 767-200 (1982) and 767-300 (1986), 777-200 (1995) and 777-300 (1998), and the Douglas DC-10-10 (1971). Each is lighter and cheaper than the ER or -30 beside it and flies 6,500 to 11,000 km, where the long-range model crosses oceans. In an era world they arrive first, so for a year or two the only 767 or 777 anyone in the world can order is the short-range one.
  • A340-200 and A340-500. The -200 is the short A340: fewer seats than the -300 and 14,800 km of range. The -500 flies 16,670 km on four Trent 500s and burns fuel to match.
  • 737-600 and 737-700ER. The -600 is the smallest 737NG, with 132 seats and nearly 6,000 km. The -700ER reaches 10,695 km, but only with a small premium cabin: fill all 149 seats and it flies about 7,200 km.
  • A320-100 and A321-100. The first builds of each, before the extra fuel tanks: 3,300 km and 4,350 km, at a lower price than the models that followed.
  • 747-400ER. The Qantas long-range 747. 14,045 km, heavier and thirstier than the standard -400.
  • Tupolev Tu-144. A second supersonic type: 150 seats at Concorde fares, 3,300 km of range, and close to twice Concorde’s fuel burn. It is in the game because it flew, not because it pays.

Each one sits on a production line with its siblings (the 777s on the 777 line, the A340s on the A330 and A340 line), so in a production-line world they share that line’s delivery slots with every other airline. Each shares a maintenance family with its siblings too, so adding a 767-200 to a 767 fleet does not start a new family, and each has its own outline in the cabin designer. The Tu-144, like Concorde, cannot be leased.

November 11, 2026

Hubs sell the connections that make sense

A focus city at New York with 147 routes was carrying one connecting passenger a week. Two things were wrong, and both are fixed in every world from the next game week.

  • The wrong markets were on sale. Every pair of routes at a hub is a possible connection, and a hub can only work through so many each week, so it keeps a shortlist. The shortlist was ranked by how many people travel between the two cities and nothing else. The busiest pairs among any big hub’s destinations are neighbours of each other: Tokyo and Osaka, Seoul and Tokyo, Dubai and London. New York spent its whole list offering trips like those, by way of New York, and nobody took them. The 4,000 connections that New York is on the way for were never offered.
  • On the way, or not sold. A hub now sells a connection only when the trip over the hub is no more than one and a half times the nonstop distance, the rule rival airlines’ connections already followed. What is left is ranked by how many people travel and how little the stop costs them.
  • Bigger hubs sell more. The shortlist was 150 markets whether a hub had 20 routes or 1,500. Every route into a hub now also brings its own two best connections, so the list grows with the hub and no route is left without connecting passengers because bigger cities filled it.
  • The hub card counts passengers who boarded. It used to show everyone who chose your connection, including the ones there was no seat for. Nonstop passengers board first and connecting passengers take the seats left over, so on a full network the old figure could be several times what you carried. The card, the airport page and the Statistics chart now show the passengers you flew. When many more wanted to connect than found seats, the card says how many, which is the game telling you where more seats or frequency would pay.

What changes for your airline. Revenue moves by under one percent for most airlines, because full flights were already the limit. Hubs with room on their flights gain the most. A few connections that doubled back and sold anyway, where no nonstop competed, are gone. The connecting figure on your hub card will change the most: it can fall where flights are full and rise where the old shortlist was selling nothing.

Two more fixes, in every world. Connections that use a partner airline’s flight now follow the same on-the-way rule, so they no longer sell trips that double back. And in older worlds, a route flown by several aircraft was boarding the same connecting passengers once per aircraft; each aircraft now takes its share of them. Airlines with many aircraft on their hub routes in those worlds will see connecting revenue come down to what was really carried.

New worlds seat connections differently. Until now nonstop passengers always boarded first and connections took the seats left over, one flight at a time. On a full network that leaves a hub with almost nobody to carry, and it could board a passenger on one flight of a two-flight trip. In every world opened from today:

  • Both flights or neither. A connection is carried only when there is a seat on each of its flights, and the best fares board first.
  • A full flight can give a seat to a connection worth more. When a connecting fare is worth more than the local passengers it would displace, it takes the seat. A hub holds up to 10% of each flight this way at a Focus City, 15% at a Hub, 18% at a Major Hub and 22% at an International Gateway, so a better hub is worth more than its cost savings.
  • No route loses by it. The route that gives up the seat is paid what its local passenger would have paid, and the rest of the fare is shared between the two flights. Route Detail shows how many local passengers gave up seats, and the route planner quotes the same.

On a test airline with a full 60-route Major Hub this was worth about 3% more revenue and 12% more profit. Worlds already running keep the seating they started with.

November 10, 2026

Your airline has a page of its own, and every world has a history

The public side of the site, the part anyone can read without signing in, used to stop at a table per world. It now goes down to the airline, and back through the years.

  • A page for every airline. Each airline in a public world has its own address: its tail, its rank, a map of its network, its busiest routes with the fares it publishes, its fleet by type and its rank week by week. Click any airline in the Worlds tables to see one.
  • Share airline. A new button in the top bar of the game copies the link to your airline’s page. Pasted into Discord or a group chat it opens as a card with your tail, your rank and the size of your network. The card is redrawn each game week. It shows rank, not profit.
  • The season so far. Every world has a history page: who climbed and who fell over the last 52 weeks, who went under, the largest orders, and the top three airlines at the close of every game year since the world opened. The yearly table is permanent, so a year you finished on the podium stays on the record.
  • Hall of fame. One page for the airline leading each world and how long it has held first place, the standing records (largest fleet, most passengers in a week, longest run at number one), the dates each season ends, and the trophies handed out in events.
  • The tables show what they are ranked on. The public standings listed market value and cash, neither of which decides the order. They now show average weekly profit, the figure the rank is made from, with the move since last week beside each rank, the airline’s logo, and a map of where everyone is based. Cash is no longer published.

What these pages do not show. They are about airlines, not the people running them. Your display name, your account, your badges and when you last played do not appear on them, and the game no longer hands those details to anyone who is not signed in. Inside the game nothing changes: other players in your world see what they always have. Private and supporter worlds have no public pages at all.

November 10, 2026

Write your timetable by typing it

Setting a timetable in Operations → Schedule used to mean dragging each flight, or pressing +15 minutes until it got there, once for every flight of the week. You can now type it.

  • One flight. Click a flight and type when it leaves, in local time at the airport it leaves from. 07:30, 0730, 19 and 7pm are all understood.
  • A whole week at once. Under that, the route’s week is laid out as rows: a time, and the days it flies at that time. Change the times, tick or untick days, add a row for a flight that only runs at weekends, then press Set timetable. Fourteen flights a week is two times, typed once.
  • It says no clearly. Two departures too close together for the aircraft to get back are refused, and you are told the earliest time that would work. In worlds with hub banks, that round trip includes the time the aircraft waits at the far end for its bank. If a time is already taken by a flight you set on another route, that day’s flight leaves at the nearest free time and the panel tells you which one.
  • Banks still show. A flight typed off a bank is marked the same way as one dragged off it, and Snap to the bank still puts it back.

What a timetable does for your airline has not changed. In worlds with hub banks your times decide who connects; everywhere else they set the departure board and the planes on the globe.

November 10, 2026

Fare and schedule changes no longer freeze the page

The second part of today’s speed work, for the largest airlines. When you change a fare, a frequency or anything else about a route you already fly, the page now takes the change at once and works out your new figures in the background. While it does, a small dot shows beside the clock and the profit and load figures on screen are still the previous ones. They catch up a moment later.

  • The biggest airline in Scarce Assets (about 4,900 aircraft): the longest freeze after a fare change went from 1.7 seconds to 0.3 on the Dashboard, from 1.2 to 0.5 on Routes and from 0.7 to 0.2 on Fleet.
  • An airline of about 1,900 aircraft: no pause after a fare change is longer than a sixth of a second.
  • Rivals’ moves. When another airline’s change reached your page, the largest airlines froze for a second and a half on the Dashboard. That is now about a third of a second.

The figures you end up with are exactly the ones you would have had before; only the wait is different. Opening or closing a route and buying or selling aircraft still pause the way they did.

November 10, 2026

Big airlines: clicks, new weeks and the Fleet page are several times faster

The larger your airline, the longer the game froze after every click: two seconds or more on the biggest airlines in Lunar Gateway and Scarce Assets, and longer still on the Fleet page. This update is about that. It applies to every world, including ones already running.

  • Clicks. Changing a fare or a schedule on an airline of about 1,900 aircraft froze the page for 2 to 2.5 seconds. It now takes about half a second. On an airline of 870 aircraft it went from about 0.8 seconds to between a quarter and half a second.
  • New weeks. When a week landed, every open tab sent the server a note that its pop-ups had been read, and the page froze while that was answered: 2 to 4 seconds on a big airline. Your browser now remembers which pop-ups it has shown and which weekly debrief you closed, and nothing is sent. A new week lands in about half a second.
  • The Fleet page. It used to build a row for every aircraft you own. With 1,900 aircraft it took 6 seconds to open, and with 4,900 nearly a minute. It now builds only the rows on screen and opens in half a second or less.
  • The server. Every decision made the server work out every airline’s view of every rival again, about a second in the largest world, while everyone else’s requests waited. It now does that once a minute. The weekly tick’s sums for the biggest airlines are two to four times quicker as well.

None of the game’s numbers changed. Fares, demand and profits come out exactly as they did before.

Three things to know. On fleets of more than 150 aircraft your browser’s find-in-page only sees the rows on screen, so use the search box above the list. The first time you open a world on a new device you may see the last few weeks’ pop-ups once. And the very largest airlines, at 4,000 routes and more, still wait a second or two per click: that is next.

November 9, 2026

The Advisor, a briefing for your own AI, and AI connectors for supporters

A new page under Company reads your airline the way an experienced manager would and tells you what to do about it. In every world, including ones already running.

  • The Advisor. Company ▸ Advisor lists what needs attention, most urgent first. Each tip says why, names the routes or aircraft involved, and has a button to the screen that fixes it. It looks for idle aircraft (with a button that opens the Route Finder on that type), aircraft with hours to spare, routes that are full but losing money, routes flying mostly empty, routes selling out, heavy checks coming due with nothing booked, gates nearly full, crew pay under the going rate while you’re short-handed, unfunded marketing, unhedged fuel, loans you could clear from cash, and no ancillaries policy.
  • It won’t give bad advice on young routes. Routes in their first 16 weeks are left out while demand builds. If your maintenance team is paid well enough to book its own heavy checks, the Advisor doesn’t nag you to book them. Its figures are the same projection the Dashboard uses, so the two never disagree.
  • One line on the Dashboard shows how many suggestions there are and the most urgent one.
  • Copy airline briefing. One button copies your airline as plain text: cash and recent profit, the Advisor’s tips, your best and worst routes with fares, your fleet, gates and settings. Paste it into Claude, ChatGPT or any AI chat and ask it anything about your airline. Download .md saves the same text as a file. It covers your own airline only, and the game sends it nowhere.
  • The briefing knows its way around. It now ends with where every page is, in the classic layout and the map view, and each tip names its exact screen (Finance ▸ Planning ▸ Fuel, not just Finance). Your AI can tell you exactly where to click.
  • Connect your own AI (supporters). Supporters can create a connector in the lobby and paste its link into Claude, ChatGPT or any app that takes an MCP connector. Their AI then reads the latest figures for every airline they fly, in more detail than the briefing. It is read-only: it can suggest, never change a fare, route or order. It sees only the kind of information the free briefing shows, so it gives no edge over anyone.
  • Why connectors are a supporter thank-you. Every question an AI asks makes the server read the airline from the database: several MB a time for a big airline, on the same servers that run every world. Supporter tips pay that bill. The game never calls an AI itself, so players only use their own AI subscription.

The in-game Wiki has a new Advisor & AI briefing section. For what the route figures mean, see the route economics guide.

November 6, 2026

Demand model v4: frequency sells seats, and every world has a business cycle (new worlds)

New worlds now run demand model v4. It keeps v3’s real-world demand and seasons, and changes how the market answers what you do. Look for the “frequency sells seats” tag in the lobby. Worlds already running keep the rules they started with.

  • Frequency sells seats. On a route you fly alone, how often you fly now changes how many people travel. Daily service is the baseline. On a 500 km business route, once a week sells about 30% fewer seats, four times a week about 13% fewer, and twice daily about 19% more, up to 25% at most. Leisure travellers care less, and on long haul the gain stops at about daily.
  • Out-flying a rival counts for more. Flying three times a rival’s schedule now wins about 80% of the business travellers, up from about 70%.
  • Pricing headroom you earn. On a market no rival flies, and where no rival serves both airports, you can charge more before passengers start to walk. Your hub at either end adds a little more, by hub size. Past that point the fall-off is gentler. The planner’s fare-cliff warnings use the same figures.
  • The A380 and 747-8 need code-F stands. They can only use 77 big gateway airports, and opening, reassigning and both route planners respect that. Freighters aren’t affected.
  • Flat gate rent. Each extra gate used to cost more than the last. Rent is now flat by airport tier, with a big-tenant discount: 10% off gates 11–25 and 20% off from gate 26 on.
  • A business cycle. Each world has its own booms and slowdowns, about four game years from peak to peak, usually moving demand 3–5% either way (never more than 15% down or 12% up). New lease rates move with it. Everyone in the world shares the same cycle, and a Dashboard card shows where it is, which way it’s heading, and today’s demand and lease rates. Demand events come about half as often but hit harder.
  • Meltdowns are earned. An operational meltdown can only happen if you fly your fleet past 85% of its hours (more likely with no spare aircraft) or let pilot, ground or cabin morale fall below 55. Stay clear of both and the risk is zero. A meltdown costs satisfaction and 3 points of reputation, and makes the world news. Airlines with fewer than 5 aircraft are exempt.
  • Board mandates. From the second quarter the board sets one goal a quarter, picked from where your airline stands: passengers, margin, load factor, satisfaction, a new country or new routes. Meet it for a $1.5M bonus, scaled to the era. The card stays on your Dashboard after the starter objectives are done.

In every world, including ones already running: the 787 and A350 families now cruise at Mach 0.85 (900 km/h) with corrected fuel burn, so the 787-9 and A350-900 come much closer to the best aircraft for their missions.

November 6, 2026

The jet age, and the Original 737s (era worlds)

In era worlds, propliners never went out of fashion: an airline could reach the late 1960s flying only Vanguards and lose nothing for it. Real passengers left propellers for jets fast once the jets arrived. Now they do here too.

  • Props lose passengers to jets on longer flights. The jet age ramps in from 1958 and reaches full strength in 1965. On sectors over 400 km, a propeller airliner (turboprops and pistons) loses demand, up to 35% at 1,600 km and beyond. Short hops are untouched, and freighters are exempt.
  • It costs you fare more than seats. In a deep market a prop still fills; you just can’t charge as much. Against a jet at the same fare, a fully affected prop keeps about 40% of the passengers.
  • You can see it. Route Detail shows a red “Jet age” line with the demand lost on affected routes, and forecasts and the Route Planner include it.
  • New: Boeing 737-100 (1968), Lufthansa’s launch type: 103 seats in a typical layout (124 at most) and 2,850 km of range. Its line closed in 1969 after 30 were built, and as a brand-new type it can only be ordered, not leased.
  • The 737-200 now enters service in 1968 (was 1971), on the same “Boeing 737 Original” production line.

This applies to every era world, including ones already running, from the next week. Classic worlds aren’t affected. More on the type in the aircraft guide.

November 6, 2026

Design your cabins: a floor plan and seat products

Refitting an aircraft is now something you can see and shape.

  • See every seat. The refit screen draws your aircraft from above with every seat on it. Paint cabins onto the floor with a brush, or drag the dividers between them.
  • Choose the seat in each cabin. Economy: 28″, 30″, 32″ or 34″ pitch. Premium Economy: 36″, 38″ or 40″, with optional leg rests. Business: recliner, cradle (from 1990), angled lie-flat (1996), fully flat (2000) or a suite with a door (2014). First: recliner, sleeper (1980), suite (1998) or, on widebodies, an apartment (2014).
  • Add extras. Seatback screens (from 1992), seat power (2005), direct aisle access in Business (2006, drawn 1-2-1), an onboard bar on widebodies (1970) and a shower suite on double-deckers (2008).
  • Bigger seats take more floor. Tight 28″ economy packs in more rows; lie-flats and suites take more space, so picking one shrinks that cabin to fit. The floor plan redraws as you go.
  • Products reach the right travellers. What you fit in Business and First wins business travellers; what you fit in Economy wins leisure travellers. The preview shows the quality each group sees.
  • They cost money. Premium seats carry a fitting fee per seat (a fully flat bed $40k, a Business suite $80k), the bar and shower are priced per aircraft, and products add a little to running costs. Downgrades are free. The aircraft is out of service while it’s refitted.
  • Service grade (basic, standard, premium or luxury) can now be set on the refit screen too. Before, a refit quietly reset it to standard.

Era and airframe limits apply: no lie-flats in 1985 and no shower on a 787, and the screen says why. More on cabins in the fleet planning guide.

November 6, 2026

Smaller touches: route width, load-factor colours, a profit waterfall and more

  • Route width by passengers. Turn on Width on the Network map (flat map, globe or map view) and busy routes draw thicker. Off by default.
  • Colour routes by load factor. A Profit / Load factor switch on the Network map colours routes in five bands from cool to hot, with a key. Planes on the globe follow it, and it’s remembered in your browser.
  • Where the money went. A compact profit waterfall on the Dashboard, above the Weekly P&L: revenue, then flying, fleet, staff, gates and HQ, brand, other, and loans and tax, down to net cash. It shows last week, or this week’s projection before your first week, and links to the full version in Finance.
  • Week-on-week arrows on the Dashboard’s load factor, passengers, yield and fleet.
  • Rank arrows. Rankings show how far each airline has moved since last week.
  • Season progress. A bar over the lobby standings, and a compact one in the top bar, show how far through the world you are, with the final year shaded.
  • Live side races above the standings: best newcomer (scored per week flown), regional champions (regions with two or more airlines) and busiest this week.
  • Your place in the queue. In production-line worlds, each order under Fleet → On Order shows how many aircraft are ahead of it on its line, counting every airline’s bookings.
  • World rule presets. When creating a world, start from Standard, Hardcore, Sandbox or Classic. Change any switch and it becomes Custom.

Freight bulk pricing

Freight pricing on Routes → Freight now works the way passenger pricing does.

  • Tick and reprice. Tick lanes (or Select all) and a bar appears: change the rate by a percentage with a live preview, including how many freighters would lose 5% or more of their freight at the new price; reset to the going rate; change flights a week; or close them.
  • ⚖️ Bulk Pricing. Filter your lanes by load factor and by how your rate compares with the going rate, then reprice them by a percentage or reset them to the going rate.
  • Edit the rate in the row. The yield column is now an editor: a stepper for each freighter, and −/+ for the whole lane where several of your freighters share it.

Hub banks: connections come from your timetable (new worlds)

Until now every connection at your hub was assumed to work, with a flat 1.5-hour wait, whenever the flights actually ran. In new worlds, hubs run on banks, the way real ones do.

  • Waves of arrivals, then departures. Every hub you designate, your focus city included, runs in banks: flights land together, passengers change, and flights leave together.
  • Connections come from the timetable. A connection works when the inbound lands 40 minutes to 6 hours before the onward flight leaves. How many of the possible connections actually work, and the real wait, decide how well it competes with a rival’s nonstop or connection. A pair your timetable never connects sells no connecting tickets.
  • The automatic schedule banks for you. You don’t have to touch anything. Each hub gets 3 banks a day (4 at bigger hubs, 6 at the biggest). Aircraft leave on a departure wave and wait at the far end, up to 4 hours (or overnight for the day’s first bank), to come back for the next arrival wave.
  • Or set your own for each hub in Operations → Schedule: 3, 4 or 6 banks, your own times (up to 8, at least 90 minutes apart, in hub local time), or Rolling with no banks.
  • See how it’s working. Each hub card shows a 24-hour strip of arrivals and departures with the banks shaded, connections a week, the average wait, connecting passengers last week and how long your aircraft wait for banks. Flights you’ve moved off a bank get an amber underline, and Snap to the bank (one flight or all of them) puts them back.

You don’t need the schedule editor for any of this: the automatic banks connect nearly as many passengers as the old assumption did, while a hub left on Rolling connects far fewer. Hub banks are on by default when you create a world, and those worlds show a 🕓 chip in the lobby. Worlds already running keep the old rules. More in the hub strategy guide.

Priority delivery replaces the early-slot fee (production-line worlds)

The old early-slot fee only ever moved your first aircraft forward, and on a busy line it bought nothing at all, because the near weeks were already taken. Priority delivery replaces it.

  • A fifth of every line’s weekly output is held back for priority orders (none on lines that build only one or two a week). Standard orders fill the rest, and the held-back slots open to everyone from the standard delivery week.
  • Priority aircraft arrive a week closer together: every 2 weeks for narrowbodies, 3 for widebodies, 4 for the A380. Still no four A380s in a month.
  • The fee is priced by weeks skipped, aircraft by aircraft, up to 50% of list. On a quiet line most aircraft after the first cost little extra; on a packed line you pay more, but you really do jump the queue.
  • At checkout, choose Standard · list price or ⚡ Priority. Each shows its first delivery week, spacing and total fee, and market cards show the next priority week and its fee.

This applies to every world with production lines, including ones already running, so standard buyers there now get four-fifths of each week’s output until the week is close. Thanks to Barca on Discord for spotting the problem.

Freight now connects through your airports

Until now a freighter route only carried freight between its own two cities. Fly Hong Kong–Anchorage and Anchorage–Chicago and you got nothing from Hong Kong–Chicago freight, even though that is exactly how real freight networks work. Now:

  • Freight changes aircraft wherever two of your freighter routes meet. You don’t need to make the airport a hub, so an Anchorage-style transit stop works as well as your home base.
  • A route’s own freight loads first. Connecting freight only fills payload the route’s own freight leaves empty, so no route loses freight it already carries. A route with spare payload now has something to fill it with, which can make an extra frequency pay.
  • Only sensible routings. Freight won’t take a detour of more than about 30%, so Hong Kong–Tokyo freight never goes via Dubai, and the more direct the connection, the more of the market it wins.
  • It is contested. A nonstop freighter on the same market, whether yours or a rival’s, takes the bigger share. Pricing your legs above the going rate wins less connecting freight, just as it does on your routes’ own freight.
  • Revenue is split between the two flights by distance, and freight changing aircraft pays a handling charge at the connecting airport.
  • You can see it. Freight routes that carry connecting freight show a green “connecting” tag. Hover over it for the markets and where they connect. The freight summary shows how much of your freight revenue is connecting, and the freight Route Planner shows what a new route would pick up and what it adds to the routes it connects with.

This applies to every world, including ones already running. More on building a network in the how-to-play guide.

October 31, 2026

City loyalty: frequent flyers you have to win (new worlds)

The loyalty program has been rebuilt for new worlds. The old weekly budget is gone; members are now something you win from other players, city by city.

  • Frequent flyers live in cities. Every city has its own frequent flyers, and every airline flying there competes for them. The more you fly from a city and the better your program, the more of them carry your card. Most people carry no card at all unless a program is worth it.
  • What makes a program worth joining is mostly what your airline already is: how generous it is, a lounge in that city your members can use, a free checked bag, premium cabins, an alliance, how many places you fly from the city, travel partners working there, and being on time.
  • Loyalty only decides which airline people fly. It never adds travellers to a market. On a route nobody else flies it changes nothing; where you compete, your members choose you and shrug off a rival’s cheaper fare.
  • One setting: generosity, the share of the fare members earn back (3–12%). Raising it is free. Cutting it is a devaluation: members walk out, your cities shop around for 8 weeks, and your reputation takes a hit.
  • Status match. When a rival devalues, you have 3 weeks to honour its members’ status in the cities you both fly and take half of the ones walking out. Both the devaluation and the match go on the world news.
  • The status year. Every December elites requalify against the flying you kept up in their city. Cut a city’s routes and its elites leave with them. Elites also leave unreliable airlines first.
  • See it on the map. Turn on the Loyalty layer on the Network map (flat or globe) to see who holds each city’s frequent flyers. The Loyalty page lists every city, your share against your biggest rival, and what your program is worth there.

Worlds created before today keep the budget-and-tier program.

Real-world demand goes global (new worlds)

Yesterday’s demand overhaul checked the game against real traffic in the US and Australia. New worlds now get the same treatment almost everywhere else. We compared passenger demand with official route statistics from the EU, the UK, Switzerland, Norway, Turkey, Brazil, Mexico, India and Japan, and every international route to and from the US and Australia:

October 30, 2026

Markets that grow with service (new worlds)

Until now every city pair had a fixed size, whoever flew it and however often. In new worlds a market now grows with the service it gets, and fades when that service leaves:

  • Every market starts thin. A city pair nobody flies is 70% of its full size, and a new world starts with all of them there. Fly a pair and its market builds over the following year: about twice a day at the going fare brings it to full size (daily gets it to about 90%), and more flights or lower fares grow it further, up to 30% above normal. This replaces the old 16-week ramp-up.
  • Markets are shared. Every airline’s flights on a pair count toward its size, so the first airlines into a market build it for everyone. Your frequency, brand and product still decide who carries the passengers, and a newcomer on a busy route wins its share gradually instead of shrinking the whole market.
  • Markets remember. Cut a route and its market fades over months, not overnight. Whoever moves in soon after takes over most of what was built.

Market development is on by default for new worlds, and the world list shows it as Growing markets. Worlds already running keep fixed markets.

October 29, 2026

Real-world fares and flight costs (new worlds)

Alongside the new passenger demand, new worlds now use fares and flight costs checked against real data. Real US economy fares barely rise with distance: a short hop costs nearly as much as a medium-haul trip, and coast-to-coast is far cheaper per kilometre. The old fares rose steeply with distance, so short hops were half price and coast-to-coast about 30% too dear. Old flight costs had the same flaw: they had no take-off cost, so short hops were almost free to fly.

  • Domestic fares follow the real curve. In every country, short domestic hops now pay more and long domestic stages less: Boston–New York goes from $93 to $117, New York–Los Angeles from $381 to $301. International fares are unchanged.
  • Every flight pays for its take-off. Fuel and crew now include a fixed amount for taxi, take-off and climb on every departure, with a lower per-kilometre rate to match. Each aircraft costs exactly what it used to on its typical mission (about 1,200 km for a narrowbody, 6,000 km for a widebody), so short hops cost more and long stages a little less.
  • Gate to gate takes longer. Each flight now includes taxi and climb time on the block-hour clock, so an aircraft flies fewer short hops a week: about 44 Boston–New York round trips for an A320neo, down from 58. Schedules use the same times.

The result: short-haul is no longer the easy money it was, and long-haul international is unchanged. Long domestic routes in a single-class cabin are a bit harder, so they reward premium seats and the right-sized aircraft, much as they do for real airlines. Like the demand changes, this goes about two-thirds of the way to the real numbers. Worlds already running are unchanged. Added to Tailwinds as well (new games).

October 29, 2026

Passenger demand recalibrated against real traffic (new worlds)

We checked passenger demand against real ticket data: every US city pair in the US Department of Transportation’s airfare survey and every Australian domestic route in the government’s route statistics. The big routes were close, but the game had about four times as many US domestic passengers as the real world, almost all of them in small cities and short hops. Australia had only about half its real traffic. New worlds now use a recalibrated model:

  • Short trips are driven, not flown. In the US and Australia, demand fades on routes under about 650 km.
  • Small towns lose travellers to big airports nearby. A small US airport within a few hours’ drive of a major one has a smaller market of its own.
  • US city size matters more. Small US cities generate fewer trips per person, so regional routes are thinner.
  • Theme-park and casino cities are sized more realistically. Most visitors to places like Orlando and Las Vegas drive there, so they count for less air demand than visitors to island resorts like Hawaii.
  • Australia flies more. Long domestic routes like Sydney–Melbourne and Brisbane–Cairns get substantially more demand.
  • Seasons follow real traffic. Every US and Australian airport now has its own seasonal pattern, taken from three years of real passenger numbers. Florida and Arizona are busiest in winter, Seattle, Boston and Chicago in summer. Ski towns like Vail and Aspen do most of their year between December and March, and Darwin and Cairns peak in the dry season.
  • Regions fly differently. Westerners fly far more than the old model gave them credit for, and the Northeast and Midwest fly a little less. On top of that, each US and Australian airport is matched to its real traffic, and airports we have no data for take their region’s figure.
  • Busy short-haul corridors. Short hops are now judged on real driving times rather than distance, so a mountain pass counts for what it is. California, the Pacific Northwest and the Texas Triangle fly far more short-haul than other regions. The New York–Philadelphia–Washington corridor loses travellers to the train, and Floridians drive. Mountain resorts like Aspen, Jackson Hole and Sun Valley keep their short flights, because nobody drives the pass if they can fly it.
  • Double-counted cities fixed. St. Petersburg, Sanford, Mesa, Newport News, Everett and a few smaller airports no longer carry their whole metro’s population a second time.

It goes about two-thirds of the way to the real numbers, so regional flying stays playable. Routes outside the US and Australia are unchanged. The fare cliff now tells the truth, and applies to every cabin

After last week’s fare cliff warnings, several of you saw routes flagged as “past the cliff” that were still flying full and earning more than at a lower fare. You were right, and there were three problems. This only affects worlds with New World Restrictions.

In a world that runs through history, nobody in 2000 wants to fly on a design from the 1950s. Until now only the age of the individual airframe counted, and that stops growing after about 13 years. So a fleet of first-generation jets flown into the 1990s cost nothing extra, and a young second-hand 707 scored almost as well as a brand-new A320. Now the age of the design counts too. The change comes across from Tailwinds.

  • Design age is a new part of the quality score. For the first 15 years after a type enters service, nothing changes. After that it loses 1 quality point a year, up to 20 points once the design is 35 years old. A 707 (in service 1958) is fine through 1973, loses 10 points by 1983 and the full 20 by 1993. This is on top of the fleet-age score, so an old airframe of an old design is hit twice.
  • It works on the calendar, not on when you bought the aircraft. A 707 delivered new in 1978 is still a 1958 design. Moving to a newer generation is what fixes it.
  • It matters most where you compete. Quality decides how the market splits between you and the other airlines on a route, human or AI, and how many business travellers you attract even where you fly alone. A rival who re-equips first will take share from you.
  • You can see it before you buy. The aircraft market shows a red Dated design badge with the current penalty, and a warning when a type is within five years of dating. Route Detail’s quality breakdown has a new Design age line, and the Fleet page shows the penalty on each aircraft.
  • There is always an up-to-date option. In every era from 1950 onwards, each aircraft category has at least one type on sale with no penalty. Concorde is the one exception: nothing replaced it, so it is never marked down.
  • Classic worlds are unchanged. They are set in a fixed 2026, so this only applies to worlds that move through the years.

August 26, 2026

Your head office now costs what your aeroplanes are worth

This started as an argument on the Discord about whether regional and island-hopper airlines are impossible to run. Rather than trade opinions we went and counted: 144 airlines across six live worlds, 105 accounts. The claim was right, and the reason turned out to be one line in the overhead formula rather than anything about the aircraft.

  • The head office was billed per airframe, not per aeroplane. Overhead counted how many aircraft you had and nothing about what they were, so two turboprops and two A380s were charged exactly the same $68,495 a week. Across the fleet, weekly revenue spans about fifty times from the smallest gauge to the largest; overhead spanned less than two. Airlines that opened on aircraft under 80 seats died at 70%, and 11 of 13 never recorded a single profitable week — against 38% for a narrowbody start. A two-turboprop startup flying one round trip a day grossed $48,734 a week against a $68,495 head office. That is 141% of gross revenue before a litre of fuel.
  • Fixed costs now follow a curve through seat count. Head-office overhead and all four crew groups read a curve anchored at the same medians every calibrated figure in the game was set against, so nothing that was already right has moved. What has gone is the cliff at each category boundary: a 290-seat 767-200ER used to pay 72% more fixed cost than a 295-seat 757-300, which had quietly handed the 757-300 the lowest break-even load factor in the game.
  • The curve now reaches the bottom of the catalogue. It used to stop at 39 seats and clamp, so a nine-seat Islander was charged a 39-seater’s head office and crew, and every type under about 21 seats lost money with every seat sold at the maximum legal frequency. Incapable, not uncompetitive — and it stranded the 25 airports in the game with runways under 4,000 feet, which exist for exactly those aircraft. A Twin Otter on the St Barths shuttle goes from −$4,275 a week to +$12,638, and now wins more missions across the route grid than any other small aircraft.
  • Who this moves, and by how much. Extending the curve to the bottom of the catalogue is a pure reduction — nothing under 39 seats got dearer. Replacing the category steps with a curve cuts both ways: of the airlines we measured, 19 are better off and 26 slightly worse, the largest rise being 1.85% of revenue for a fleet of five double-deckers and the largest fall 6.5% for a fleet of 164 turboprops. Everyone in a world moves at the same moment, so relative standings should hold broadly steady.
  • Small aircraft still ask something of you. The small operators who were already making money were all doing the same thing — very high frequency on very few short routes; one flies 41 weekly frequencies on a single pair. At four times the utilisation, a turboprop’s head office falls from 141% of revenue to 35%. That lever was always there and nothing in the game taught it. It is worth a read of fleet planning before you write off a small gauge.

Crew are hired and trained now, not conjured

Labour has been a wage bill with a pay dial on it. In new worlds it is a workforce: people you recruit, pay to train, wait for, and lose if you underpay them. This is the largest change to the operating side of the game in a while, so it goes to new worlds only — switching it on under a live season would strand every airline in a world at the same moment, which is not a thing to do to people mid-season.

  • Hiring pays for training up front and delivers later. Each of the four groups has its own lead time and its own bill per crew unit: pilots ten weeks at $45,000 (a type rating and line training), maintenance six weeks at $18,000, cabin crew five weeks at $12,000, ground staff two weeks at $4,000. Ordering aircraft and ordering the people to fly them are now two different lead times you have to line up.
  • Pay is a retention decision, not just a cost. Every week a fraction of your people leave, at a rate set by what you pay and how they feel about working for you. Cutting pay to survive a bad quarter now costs you the crew as well as the morale.
  • Short-handed degrades long before it grounds. Under 15% short, the operation simply gets worse — on-time performance slips and satisfaction follows it down. At or past 15% on the flight-crew groups, aircraft you have nobody to fly stand down for the week. The crew-hungriest tails park first, so the largest possible number of aircraft keep flying, and a parked tail flies again the moment its crew arrive; nothing is written off.
  • Your first two aircraft crew instantly. Headwinds already delivers a new airline’s first two aircraft immediately, so a new competitor can start flying rather than idle for real hours waiting on metal. A ten-week pilot queue would have undone that, so the starter allowance staffs those two aircraft from the day you join.
  • Anything already flying is fully staffed. Where the system does apply to an existing airline, it starts fully crewed with nobody in training — you are never handed a staffing crisis you did not create.
  • Operations shows the whole picture. Per group: crewed against required, how far short you are, who is in training and how many weeks until they are on the line, and hire buttons that quote the bill and the wait before you commit.

The black screen, and why it happened

On Monday morning a deploy replaced every world screen with a black page — every player, every device, every browser. It lasted most of the afternoon. This is what it was.

One city, one market — and secondary airports that behave like it

A structural change in the shared engine, and one with real consequences for how you pick a base. The Tailwinds devlog covers the model detail; the short version, and what it means in a world with other airlines in it:

  • A metro area is one market. New York to London is a single pool of passengers, whether it is flown from JFK, Newark or LaGuardia. A secondary field pulls fewer passengers than the primary, by an amount that depends on the trip — LaGuardia is a fine domestic airport and a poor long-haul international one. A career that outlives the world, and codeshares that both airlines agreed to

Two days of work across a twenty-eight item audit. Most of it was engine and economy — fuel hedging, crew costs by aircraft size, union grievances, freight seasons, hub connectivity, real load factors, and a fair bit more — and because that engine is shared, it is written up on the Tailwinds devlog. It all landed here too, and some of it moves your numbers noticeably. This entry covers what changed in the multiplayer game itself.

Your record now survives the world it was set in. A season ended, the standings were computed, and then they were thrown away — they existed only inside the transaction that wrote the final week. There was a column in the database waiting for a career since the day the game launched, and nothing had ever written to it.

  • Every finished season is recorded against your account: the world, the airline you flew, where you finished, how many weeks you were in it, and your lifetime passengers carried.
  • Badges are earned from that record — champion, podium, veteran, a million passengers, and phoenix for finishing a season you went bankrupt in. They are worked out from your history every time they are shown rather than stored, so the rules can improve without anybody’s past being rewritten.
  • An airline that never went public is unranked, not last. Staying private is a decision, and recording it as “20th of 20” would misreport it. A bankrupt airline still gets its season on the record — you were there.
  • Worlds that ended before this shipped are backfilled from their final standings. Lifetime passengers for those seasons read as unknown rather than zero, because the honest answer is that the figure is years stale.

Your career panel appears under the worlds screen once you have a finished season. Before that it shows nothing at all — a hall of fame full of zeroes is worse than none.

A codeshare is a deal now, not something you do to someone. Signing one against a human rival took a fee off you and paid you interline revenue computed from a real player’s network — while that player was never asked and never told. It was the only bilateral agreement in the game that was not, in fact, bilateral.

  • You offer, they accept. Offers appear on both airlines’ Alliances tab. Accepting writes the agreement to both sides in one transaction — if either side’s engine refuses it, the whole thing is refused, because half a bilateral agreement is worse than none.
  • Offering someone who has already offered you signs it immediately. Both of you plainly consented; there is no reason to make you wait.
  • Cancelling ends both halves. Ending only your side would leave the other player paying a weekly fee for a partner who had stopped carrying their passengers.
  • Unanswered offers expire after eight weeks — not just tidiness: a dead offer sitting between two airlines would have blocked them from ever offering again.

An airline that fails now leaves something behind for everyone else. Bankruptcy was a status change and a single news item. The fleet froze inside a save nobody would read again, the orderbook stopped existing, and the gates went back to the pool in silence — and only on gate-scarcity worlds, because that was the one branch anybody had written. A carrier could go under with forty aircraft and a dozen slots at a fortress hub and leave the world exactly as it found it.

  • The fleet goes to the used market at a distressed price. An administrator sells into a market that knows it has to, and the listing names the airline that failed.
  • The gates are listed as administrator’s sales on worlds where gates are scarce, priced off each airport’s own auction reserve — a Heathrow slot is not valued like a regional one.
  • Nobody gets paid. The money leaves the world rather than being credited to a company that no longer exists. Every check that protects you as the buyer still applies.

A rival going under should be an opportunity for everyone still flying, and now it is.

The interest rate on your loans is no longer the client’s to choose. A modified client could ask for the 3% floor on a loan that should have cost 18%, and the server would write it down as asked — about $790,000 a year of interest that simply did not happen. Credit scoring now lives in the shared engine, computed from what your airline actually earned rather than from a forecast the server never had, and the server prices every loan itself.

Batched fleet actions are safe in multiplayer. Selling, retiring, scheduling checks or extending leases across a selection runs inside one write, with the number of aircraft capped so a huge list cannot hold row locks while everyone else waits. If one aircraft cannot be actioned it is skipped and reported rather than aborting the batch — seven of twelve checks scheduled before the money ran out is the honest outcome.

Brand awareness sells seats now, instead of quietly changing your fares

A player asked a good question: his margins were thin when he started, but months later brand-new routes were printing 37–70% margins at the reference price, and his revenue had climbed steadily for a year while he opened no routes and changed no prices. He was right that this made no sense — revenue cannot rise when neither your price nor your load factor moves. It was a bug, and it was one of the oldest in the engine.

Brand awareness, reputation, your loyalty programme and your alliance were being multiplied onto route revenue — after the aircraft was already full. They moved no passengers at all. A brand-new airline wasn’t reaching 45% of its market, as intended; it was selling every seat at 45% of its own ticket price. That is why your first routes lost money with full cabins, and why raising fares felt like the only way out. Worse, the payout was biggest on routes already at 100% load — exactly where a stronger brand cannot sell another seat.

All of it now works the way it reads:

  • Your brand wins passengers, not fares. On a route you have to yourself, an airline nobody has heard of simply reaches less of the market — those travellers drive, connect elsewhere, or don’t go. On a contested route, they go to your rival. Either way you fly emptier aircraft at the price on the ticket, instead of full aircraft at a price you never set.
  • Revenue ÷ passengers is now your actual fare. It wasn’t before. Per-cabin revenue didn’t add up to the route total either, and the yield figure on your Finance page had been drifting upward for months on routes nobody had repriced.
  • What your brand is really worth is pricing power. Once your aircraft are full, a stronger brand doesn’t add passengers — it lets you hold a higher fare without emptying them. On a typical trunk route a well-known carrier can sustain around 1.6× the reference fare at a full aircraft where an unknown one tops out near 1.2×. If your routes are pinned at 100% load, that is the game telling you there is room in your prices.
  • Multiplayer is symmetrical again. Rivals were being scored as established brands regardless of how new they were, so in a young world two week-one airlines each treated the other as famous. They now carry their real standing.

Expect your numbers to move. A mature, well-known airline will see revenue ease back a few percent — it had been collecting a fare premium it never charged. A younger or mid-sized one will see revenue and margins improve. Nothing about your operation changed; the aircraft were always this full.

July 27, 2026

Capital markets: your share price is the scoreboard now

Read this one before you next open your airline, because your numbers are going to move. Valuations across every live world have been rebased, and most airlines will see their market cap drop by roughly half on the next tick. Nothing has gone wrong and you haven’t lost anything — the old valuation was simply wrong, in a way that made the stock market a money printer.

Here is what was broken. An airline’s value was built on its profit multiplied by up to 32, which meant every extra $100k a week of profit added something like $166M of market cap. One good route was worth a quarter of a billion dollars. Prices then climbed toward that number at a capped 20% a week — smoothly, predictably, for weeks on end. A stock that reliably returns 20% a week against a 3% round trip isn’t an investment, it’s a vending machine.

Airlines are now valued the way real airlines are: the lowest-multiple, most cyclical business there is, on a P/E of roughly 5 to 13, with the earnings component capped against actual revenue so no valuation can outrun the business underneath it. Prices move at up to 8% a week instead of 20%.

The market moves, not just your airline

Every world now has a market index: one shared sentiment number that lifts or depresses every airline’s valuation together, drifting through bull and bear phases, and levered to the fuel price. When fuel spikes, the whole sector re-rates down. A rising share price can now mean the market rose rather than that you did well — and a genuinely good quarter can be swamped by a bad market. Timing matters.

You’re ranked on value per share

This is the real change, and it is the reason for everything else. The world standings used to rank on market cap. Market cap measures how big you got, and that quietly made the game reward the wrong things: hoarding cash raised your score, and returning a single dollar to your shareholders lowered it. Every dividend, every buyback, was a self-inflicted wound on the leaderboard. No sane player would ever pay one.

Standings now rank on shareholder value per share — your share price, plus every dividend you have ever paid. It measures what you created for each unit of ownership rather than how much empire you accumulated. Growing a big, profitable airline is still the dominant way to win. What changed is that capital allocation is no longer free.

Your rank will not jump the day this lands: every airline is on the same share count at the moment of the change, so the new ordering starts out identical to the old one. It only begins to diverge as players start issuing and retiring shares.

Your company, on the Stocks tab

There’s a new Your company panel, and four decisions in it that didn’t exist before.

  • Go public. New airlines are now incorporated private: no tradable shares, nobody on your register but you, and no place in the standings. From week 26, with a trading record behind you, you can list — selling 10–35% of the company to investors and putting the cash in your treasury. IPOs price at a discount, and the discount is smaller the longer and more profitably you have traded. Staying private is a legitimate way to play. It just isn’t a way to win.
  • Share offerings. Raise more later, up to 15% of your shares a game year. The discount widens each time you go back to the market, and narrows if you have a record of returning capital — being good to your shareholders buys you cheaper money later.
  • Buybacks. Retire stock from the float. Cash leaves, but the share count falls faster, so it lifts per-share value when your stock is cheap or your cash is sitting idle.
  • Dividends. A standing policy, up to 60% of trailing-quarter profit, paid every 13 weeks. Only shares held outside your founder block get paid, so a dividend costs you in exact proportion to how much of yourself you have sold. Miss a quarter and it suspends automatically — publicly.

One more thing worth knowing: idle cash is now credited at a fraction of face value. The market does not reward a lazy balance sheet. Once you have run out of gates to take and aircraft to order, a pile of unspent cash actively drags on your valuation, and returning it beats sitting on it. That is precisely the point at which real airlines start paying dividends.

There is a real person on the other side of your trade

Trades used to be filled by nobody. Selling shares conjured cash into the world out of thin air, which is why late-game worlds turned into a glut of money. Every world now has a finite float pool of outside-investor cash and shares. Your buys pay into it, your sells draw out of it, and IPOs and offerings are funded from it. As it runs down, sellers get worse fills; if it empties, the equity window shuts and you cannot raise capital at all, because there is no money on the other side. It heals slowly. First mover to the market gets the cheap capital.

Trading is also properly frictional now. A large order moves the price against itself in proportion to how much of the free float it represents, so a big stake can no longer be accumulated at the marked price, and realized gains are taxed at 25%. Reading a rival’s numbers and backing your judgement still pays. It just isn’t free money any more.

If the re-rating looks wrong on your airline, or the new standings read oddly to you, say so in Discord. This is the largest change to how Headwinds is scored since launch and I would rather hear about it early.

One world, one calendar

Players kept reporting that they and their rivals were seeing different dates in the same world, and they were right. This one is worth explaining, because it was doing more damage than a wrong number in the top bar.

A new airline was always created at Year 1, Week 1, no matter what the world clock said. Join a world sitting at Year 3, Week 12 and you didn’t join it in the spring of year three — you started your own private January and ran it alongside everyone else’s calendar for the rest of the game. Because the simulation reads the season off your week, that wasn’t cosmetic: you were flying a different season to your rivals in the same market, catching the summer peak on transatlantic routes while the carrier competing with you was in the depths of February. Anything scheduled by date — aircraft deliveries, fuel hedges, the quarterly rhythm — was keyed to your calendar rather than the world’s.

  • You now join in the world’s here-and-now. A late joiner lands on the current world date, in the current season, on the same week as everyone else.
  • Existing worlds have been corrected. Airlines already running on a private calendar were rebased onto the world clock, with anything scheduled ahead of them shifted to match, so nothing pending was lost in the move.
  • “Joined at” means something now. Your join date used to record the week of the year and throw the year away — someone joining in year three was filed as having joined at week 12. It records the real point in the world’s history.

And the December Week 4 freeze

The same investigation turned up the cause of a bug several of you had reported and none of us could pin down: the game appearing to stop dead at December, Week 4, with ticks landing on the server but nothing moving on screen until you reloaded the page.

The client decides whether to accept a new week by checking that it’s later than the one on screen — and it was comparing the week of the year. Every New Year, week 52 became week 1, which reads as time running backwards, so the client rejected the new week as stale and sat there. It now compares the actual position in the world’s history, so the year rolls over without you touching anything. If you have been reloading every December to unstick your world, you can stop.

Heavy maintenance: your fleet now visits the hangar

Aircraft don't fly forever without care. Headwinds now models the real airline maintenance calendar: your planes build up wear as they fly, and periodically they have to come out of service for a heavy check — the multi-week hangar visits every real carrier plans its fleet around. The light A- and B-checks stay folded into your weekly maintenance budget; the two big ones, the C check and the D check, are now yours to schedule.

  • Wear comes from flying, not just the calendar. Every block-hour an aircraft flies brings its next check closer. Work a jet hard nine times a day and it comes due in months; keep it as a spare and it ages on the calendar instead. A C check lands roughly every two years, a D check every six — and a D covers the C.
  • Checks take planes out of service. A C check is a week or two in the hangar; a D check is a month or more on a widebody, and it isn't cheap — cost scales with the aircraft's value. Time it for a demand trough with a spare ready to cover the route and it barely registers. Get caught in the summer peak with no cover and it hurts.
  • Schedule it your way. Open any aircraft and start a check now, or book one for a future week when the timing suits your network. The Fleet list flags every airframe — due soon, due, overdue, or in the shop.
  • Miss the deadline and the regulator acts. Blow past the grace window and your aircraft is grounded on the spot: longer than a planned check, half again as expensive, and a hit to your reputation. Fleet planning is now a real job.
  • Fresh checks pay you back. A checked airframe breaks down far less often, a D check knocks years off its running costs, and a well-kept plane holds its resale value — while one that's overdue sells at a discount, because nobody overpays to inherit someone else's deferred maintenance.
  • Your crew and hubs help. A happy maintenance team and an in-house base at one of your hubs both bring check costs down — the same standardisation that already rewards a focused fleet.

It's the call every real fleet planner faces: pour money into a 22-year-old airframe's D check, or sell it and move on. Now it's yours to make.
