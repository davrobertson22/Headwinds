Headwinds Strategy Guide

Headwinds runs the same deep simulation as its solo sibling — but the airlines across the table are people, not code. They scout you, adapt, cooperate, and occasionally act out of spite. That single fact reshapes every decision, and this guide is about playing the map and the players on it.

If you're still learning the fundamentals — hubs, fleet, routes, load factor — start with the how-to-play guide and the glossary, then come back here. And whatever you do, do it inside the Fair Play Rules: competing hard is the whole point, cheating isn't.

Scout before you fly

In a world of real opponents, information is an edge you can act on. Before you commit aircraft, use the Rivals tab, the standings and the News tab to read the room: who's already big, where their networks are concentrated, which hubs are crowded, and who's climbing fast. The Market's world order board adds what's coming: how many of each aircraft type are flying and on order across the world, and which airlines hold them. An airline that's expanding aggressively is a different threat from one sitting still on a fat home market. The map tells you where the fights will be — scout it, then choose battles you can win.

Claim territory early

Open demand is a finite resource in a shared world, and early movers lock it up. The best opening isn't the flashiest market — it's the profitable one nobody has taken yet. Establish yourself in space where you're unopposed, bank the cash, and build a base before you ever pick a fight. Attacking an entrenched rival at their fortress hub in week two is how new airlines burn their runway; growing where they aren't is how you build one big enough to attack from later.

Contested routes & pricing

When two airlines fly the same city pair, the simulation divides the passengers between them by fare, frequency, product quality, brand awareness, reputation and loyalty — and a rival flying from another airport in the same city is in the same fight. In worlds tagged Rival connections, so is a rival selling a one-stop trip over its own hub. The mechanics are familiar — but here the other operator is a person who will notice, and react. Undercut them and they may undercut back. Add frequency and they may match it. Because both sides adapt, the question isn't just "what splits this market today," but "what does my move provoke next week?"

Read the load factor in context

Load factor is still your fastest signal, but read it against who else is in the market. A route sagging to 55% because a rival just entered calls for a different response than one that was always thin. If the market can support both of you at a fair price, hold. If it can't, decide quickly whether to win it or leave it — a half-committed presence in a contested market bleeds you slowly.

Head-to-head tactics

When a rival contests a route you care about, you have three honest responses — and one trap:

  • Defend the routes central to your hub by matching fares and adding frequency, accepting a thinner margin to keep your share and your connections intact.
  • Differentiate by out-flying them rather than out-pricing them — more departures, a better cabin, a hub that connects them onward — so you win the flyers who value convenience and comfort even at a similar fare.
  • Redeploy when a route isn't worth the fight. Moving that aircraft to a market where you have the edge beats grinding two airlines into the dirt.
  • The trap is the all-out price war. Against a human, it can turn personal: a spiteful rival may torch a route's fares well past the point of profit just to hurt you, and you both lose. Sometimes the strongest play is to stop fighting and make a deal.
Against people, restraint is a weapon. The rival expecting a price war and getting a truce is off balance — and you've kept the capacity you'd have spent bleeding them.

Alliances & diplomacy

Alliances in Headwinds are founded and run by players, and the in-game messaging system is where the real game between people happens. Used well, an alliance extends your reach into markets you don't fly, feeds passengers between partners, and turns would-be rivals into a bloc. The best partners complement your network rather than overlap it — a partner who hubs where you don't doubles your effective map; one who flies your routes is just a truce waiting to break.

Diplomacy cuts both ways. You can carve up a contested region so neither of you wastes capacity fighting the other, or rally a coalition to pen in a runaway leader before they're unstoppable. But the game enforces none of it — a handshake holds only as long as both sides want it to, and a partner is always free to leave the alliance or turn on you. Codeshares are the one formal instrument: both airlines have to accept the offer, and either can cancel it, which ends it for both. Treat every deal as leverage, not law, and keep an eye on the exits.

Playing the world clock

The world advances on the server around the clock, so time management is strategy. Your network keeps flying — and your rivals keep moving — while you're offline, which rewards resilient design over constant micromanagement. Build routes that stay healthy without a hand on the wheel, keep a cash buffer so an unattended bad stretch can't end your run, and check in on a rhythm rather than in a panic. Late in a world, that same clock becomes a countdown: the title is decided on the final 52 weeks, so have your network earning before they begin, not after.

Loyalty & reputation as moats

A loyalty program and a strong brand are slow to build and, in multiplayer, hard for a rival to copy in time — which is exactly what makes them moats. Treat loyalty as a multi-year investment, strongest on the routes that touch your hubs: members enroll over months, the program's value compounds over roughly a year and a half of steady funding, and the points you award are a real future debt, so a young program looks cheaper than it is. Start early and fund it consistently and, by mid-world, you'll hold repeat flyers that a late-arriving competitor simply can't pry loose before the clock stops. Brand awareness and reputation work on the same long timescale. Steady marketing — a fixed weekly budget, or a share of revenue that grows with you — builds awareness that fades only slowly once you stop, and a good cabin product, a young fleet, a broad network and staff paid well enough to stay happy build a reputation that wins passengers at the margin and blunts a rival's undercuts — while aircraft grounded for overdue maintenance knock it back.

Finance under pressure

Cash is survival; debt is a tool — and in a live world, both are read against what your rivals might do. A thin cash balance that's fine in a quiet market becomes dangerous the moment a well-funded competitor opens a price war on your best route. Keep a deeper cushion than a solo player would, because you don't control when the pressure comes. Borrow to seize a genuine opening — a fleet for a new hub, an expansion before a rival beats you to it — but only against cash flow your existing network reliably produces. The bank holds you to that: new borrowing is capped by your recent earnings and your equity, and aircraft finance lends against owned metal valued at no more than you paid for it. Own the metal on your proven core routes to lower costs; lease for the experiments and the land-grabs — though in worlds with New World Restrictions, lessors stock only previous-generation single-deck types and cap how many leased aircraft you can have on order at once, so a land-grab fleet may have to be bought.

Winning a world

Every world runs for a fixed span of game-years and ends with a final ranking, so there's an actual finish line — and everyone on the leaderboard is a person you out-flew or didn't. Play the scoreboard. The standings rank every airline still flying on its average weekly net profit over the last 52 weeks, and the title goes to the best average over the final 52 — listed or private alike, so going public is a financing decision, not an entry ticket. That makes a world's last year a season of its own: track your 52-week average against the field, consolidate your strongest markets as it approaches, and resist the classic blunder of a huge, debt-fuelled expansion inside that window, where every new route's slow first weeks, every extra lease and every interest payment comes straight off the number that decides the title. The airline that finishes on top is usually the one that claimed good ground early, defended it intelligently, partnered wisely, and grew at a pace it could always digest.

Put it together and the playbook is clear: scout the field, take open territory before others do, fight only the battles worth winning, use alliances to extend and to contain, protect your position with loyalty and brand, keep a balance sheet that can absorb a rival's worst move, and steer toward the final standings the whole way. Do that better than the people around you, and you'll finish a world at the top of the table.
