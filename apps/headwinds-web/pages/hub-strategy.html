Territory: Hub Strategy on a Shared Map

In solo airline games, a hub is an engineering project. In Headwinds it's a land claim. Every airport on the map is available to every player in the world, your rivals can see where you've planted your flag, and the best hub you'll ever have is one another player wanted too — but you got there first, dug in, and made attacking it pointless. This is a guide to thinking about hubs as territory.

The opening: claim before you optimize

A fresh world is a brief window where the map is empty and every player is making the same decision at once. Your starter fleet arrives instantly — Headwinds hands you your first two aircraft with no delivery wait, and crews them from day one, precisely so these opening moves happen at speed. Use that speed. The perfect spreadsheet-optimal hub chosen in week three is worth less than a very good hub claimed in week one, because by week three the very good hubs are taken.

What makes a claim defensible isn't raw airport size — it's the mismatch between how much the location is worth to you and how much it would cost a rival to contest. A dense catchment of nearby cities you can ring with short spokes, solid local demand to earn from before connections spin up, and no other player already committed there: that combination lets a modest airline become locked-in faster than a giant can be dislodged from anywhere.

Check the News tab and the standings before you commit — the join form even tells you how many airlines are already based at the airport you type. If two players have already opened routes from the airport you're eyeing, you're not choosing a hub — you're choosing a battlefield.

Gates are the deed

A claim is only as real as the gates behind it. A gate handles up to 50 departures a week, every route uses slots at both of its ends, and an airport can't be designated as a hub at all until you hold enough gates there — five for a focus city, ten for a full hub, more for each tier above. In a standard world gates never run out, but each extra gate at the same airport costs more than the last, up to a ceiling, so a dominant presence at a big airport is genuinely expensive to hold.

Worlds created with gate scarcity (the ⛩ badge in the lobby) make the land claim literal. Every airport has a fixed number of gates, and once they're leased they're gone. No airline may hold more than 60% of an airport's gates and no alliance more than 80%, and everyone is guaranteed five at their own home hub — enough for a focus city, not a fortress. Once an airport is full, the ways in are a yearly sealed-bid auction for new gates, a rival willing to sell on the gate market, or gates someone else lets go — and gates at an airport you stop flying to are forfeited after 24 weeks. In those worlds the gates you hold at a busy airport are the hub; the aircraft are just what you put through them.

What a hub designation buys

Connecting traffic only exists where you've built for it. Passengers change planes between your own flights only at an airport you've designated: the airport you founded at starts as a Hub, and anywhere else is designated from the Hubs screen. There are four tiers — Focus City, Hub, Major Hub and International Gateway. Each step up needs more gates and more routes at the airport, a construction bill and a bigger weekly running cost; the top two also need international destinations, and a Gateway must have run as a Major Hub for half a year and be moving real connecting volume. In return the airport becomes a better place to change planes: passengers mind the connection less, more of the airport's transfer traffic finds your flights, every route touching it scores higher on quality, station and layover costs there fall, and each gate takes more departures before the hub congests. A full hub must be in your home country; abroad you're limited to focus cities, one per country.

Why fortresses hold

A developed hub defends itself through the connecting bank. Once a dozen spokes exchange passengers through your schedule, each spoke earns from dozens of onward markets that a point-to-point invader can't touch. A rival entering one of your spokes competes for that pair's local travellers only — while your aircraft on the same pair also carries everyone connecting to the rest of your network. You can profitably match a fare that loses them money, indefinitely. Most players learn to read this. The ones who don't, fund your next fleet order.

This is also why the scariest attack on a hub isn't a route entry at all — it's a rival building their own hub one catchment over, then bleeding your outer spokes at their edges. In worlds with rival connections switched on — the default for new worlds — that's literal: a rival's designated hub sells one-stop journeys over it, in the same markets your connections serve and on pairs you fly nonstop. Watch the map for construction, not just for fare cuts. The route economics guide covers the pricing mechanics of contested pairs; the short version is that geography wins arguments that fares can't.

Mega-hubs: the crowded crown

Every world has a handful of marquee airports that attract multiple players the way a gold rush attracts prospectors, and for the same reason: the prize is real, and so is the crowd. A shared mega-hub can work — the demand is enormous — but expect permanently contested spokes, rivals hubbing at the same airport splitting its transfer traffic with you, thinner margins than the standings suggest, and rivals with an incentive to make your life expensive. In gate-scarcity worlds the crowd also fights over a finite gate pool, and once an airport is more than 90% full everyone pays a congestion surcharge on their gates there. Nor is the airport next door an escape: a city is one market, so a rival at the sibling field competes on every pair you fly, and a secondary airport reaches fewer of the city's travellers than the main one. Entering one late, against an established player whose bank structure is already humming, is the single most reliable way to torch a treasury in Headwinds. If you want a mega-hub, want it in week one with company, or take it in the endgame from a collapsing rival — not in the middle, alone, uphill.

The second hub is a race decision

In solo play, you open a second hub when the first one's finances say you're ready. On a shared map there's a second clock: the locations worth having are disappearing. That tension — expand before you're ready and risk the death spiral, or wait and watch the last good territory get claimed — is one of the sharpest decisions in the game, and there's no formula for it. What tilts it: where you're allowed to build (a full hub has to be in your home country, so the race is for the best sites at home — abroad you can only plant focus cities), how contested your region already is (crowded worlds reward earlier claims), how defensible your first hub is without your full attention, and whether the candidate site complements your network (a second hub whose catchment overlaps your first cannibalizes your own spokes — you want new geography, connected to the old by a fat trunk route).

However you time it, fund it from proven cash flow and connect it generously to hub one. Passengers change planes once, so a trunk between your hubs is fed from both ends — every spoke at hub one can connect onto it, and so can every spoke at hub two. Two well-linked hubs reinforce each other; two isolated ones behave like two vulnerable small airlines wearing the same livery.

Reading rivals' networks

Your competitors' hub choices are open information — the Rivals tab shows every airline's fleet and route map, and ten minutes studying them beats any general principle. Things worth knowing about each neighbour: where their bank structure actually is (that's the territory they'll defend to the death), which of their routes are stray singles (those they'll abandon under pressure), and whether their expansion is drifting toward your catchment. Alliances add a diplomatic layer on top — a neighbour you can't profitably fight is often a neighbour worth a codeshare or an alliance instead, turning an overlapping border into shared feed. See rivals & alliances for when to make that trade.

Endgame: territory becomes scoreboard

When the world's final weeks arrive, hub strategy inverts. Stop planting: a new hub that matures after the world ends is charity to the history books. Start harvesting: push fares on the spokes your fortress makes uncontestable (up to the fare cliff, in worlds that have one), let marginal territory go rather than defending it at a loss, and watch for rivals who overextended — an airline that goes under has the aircraft it owned sold on the used market at a distressed price, its gates put up for sale where gates are scarce, and its markets left empty, and the standings reward whoever absorbs that traffic in the closing stretch. Your hub was never the goal. It was the machine that decides where you finish.
