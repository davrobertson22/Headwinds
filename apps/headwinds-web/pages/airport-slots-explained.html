Airport Slots Explained

At the world's busiest airports, an airline cannot simply decide to add a flight. It needs a slot: permission to take off or land at a set time. This article explains how slots are handed out and traded, why they are worth so much, and how Headwinds turns the same scarcity into a contest between players.

By the Headwinds team · Last updated November 12, 2026

What a slot is

A slot is permission to land or take off at a particular airport at a particular time on a particular day. It is not a gate, a runway or a parking stand, but the right to use all of them at that time. At most airports nobody needs one: an airline files a schedule and flies it. At the busiest airports, where runways or terminals are full for much of the day, an independent coordinator decides who may operate when, and an airline without a slot cannot schedule a flight at all.

The rules most of the world uses are IATA's Worldwide Airport Slot Guidelines. They sort airports into three levels. Level 1 airports have enough capacity and need no coordination. At Level 2 airports, airlines agree their schedules with a facilitator to avoid peaks. Level 3 airports are fully coordinated, and every flight needs a slot. In the summer 2022 season there were 156 Level 2 airports and 195 Level 3 airports, among them most of the world's largest hubs.

How slots are handed out

Slots are allocated twice a year, for the summer and winter seasons, and the most important rule is historic precedence. An airline that flew a slot last season may keep it next season, so long as it used it. That right, often called grandfather rights, means most slots at a busy airport belong in practice to the airlines that held them years ago.

The test of use is the 80/20 rule. An airline must operate a slot at least 80% of the time across the season to keep it, and a slot that falls below that goes back to a pool. In the European Union, under Regulation 95/93, half of the slots in the pool are reserved for new entrants, airlines with little or no presence at the airport. In the United States, slots at New York's JFK and LaGuardia also carry an 80% use requirement, and at Washington National a slot used less than 80% of the time over two months can be withdrawn.

Ghost flights and waivers

The use rule has a side effect: airlines sometimes fly nearly empty aircraft to keep a valuable slot. For that reason regulators suspend or loosen the rule in a crisis, as they did after the September 11 attacks, during SARS, in the 2008 recession and in the pandemic. The EU suspended it from March 2020 to March 2021, then required 50% use for winter 2021/22 and 64% for summer 2022, before the European Commission proposed a return to 80% from 30 October 2022. In the United States, the FAA has let airlines hand back up to 10% of their JFK and LaGuardia slots without losing them, through October 2026, because New York's approach control centre was staffed at only 54% of its target.

What a slot is worth

Because slots at a full airport are scarce and rarely returned, they change hands for large sums where the rules allow it. London Heathrow, which has run close to full for years, sets the benchmark. In 2008 Continental Airlines paid $209 million for four daily pairs of Heathrow slots, about $52 million a pair. American Airlines paid Scandinavian Airlines $60 million for a pair in 2015, and in February 2016 Oman Air was reported to have paid Air France–KLM $75 million for a pair, the highest price on record for a single Heathrow pair. A pair is one take-off and one landing a day, enough for a single daily round trip to somewhere else.

Slots and competition

An airline that holds most of the slots at an airport faces little competition there, and competition authorities treat slot holdings that way. When American Airlines and US Airways merged in 2013, the US Justice Department sued to block the deal partly because the combined airline would have held 69% of the take-off and landing slots at Washington National. The settlement made them give up 104 slots at Washington National and 34 at LaGuardia, along with gates at five other airports.

Critics argue the system itself protects the airlines already there. The UK Department for Transport has said the slot system was not designed to stimulate a competitive market, and in December 2018 the UK Competition and Markets Authority advised that slots could be auctioned. Others warn that auctions could push prices too high, and Europe has so far preferred to adjust the rules it has rather than replace them.

Gates and slots in Headwinds

Headwinds, our multiplayer airline game, models airport capacity with gates rather than timed slots. Each gate you lease at an airport gives you a fixed number of weekly departures there, so in practice a gate is a bundle of slots.

Every route needs a gate at both ends, and one gate handles 50 departures a week. Each airline can always lease 5 gates at its home hub, however full the airport. A slot borrowed from an alliance partner at a mega hub costs $750 a week for each weekly departure, all of it paid to the partner who owns the gate.

In a standard world gates are an unlimited rental: anyone can lease more at any airport for rent. Worlds created with gate scarcity, marked with a ⛩ badge in the lobby, work much more like a Level 3 airport. Each airport has a fixed number of gates according to its size, and once they are taken, the only ways in are the yearly auction and the market between players.

The table gives the numbers for each class. The airline and alliance caps are the most gates one airline, or all the members of one alliance together, may hold at an airport of that size. The auction reserve is the lowest bid the yearly auction accepts for one gate, about six months of its rent.

Playing a scarcity world

  • Lease early where you will grow. Gates at an airport that will fill up cost only rent today. Once it is full, the price is an auction bid or a rival's asking price.
  • Keep every gate flying. A gate you stop using goes back to the pool after 24 weeks, and you are locked out for 24 more. Sell a gate you no longer need before the clock runs out.
  • Save cash for week 40. Bids are not taken from you when you place them, but a bid you cannot pay at the new year is voided and the gate goes to the next bidder.
  • Watch the 90% line. At a congested airport everyone pays the surcharge, including the airline that filled it.
  • Lean on your alliance. Borrowing a partner's spare slots can launch a route at a full airport without a gate of your own. The owner can take them back, with four weeks' warning before your schedule is trimmed.
