**New world: Old Metal — growth should cost something**

Leasing has been too easy. Twelve weeks of rent puts an aircraft in the air, so the fastest way to grow has always been to lease, not to earn. One airline recently placed orders for 196 A380s inside two minutes. That's not a criticism — given the rules, it was the right move. So I've changed the rules in one world to see how it feels.

**Old Metal** is live now. Five things work differently.

**Lessors only carry old, single-deck aircraft.** Anything in service by 2000, plus the E-Jets, CRJ700s and current-build ATRs. No 787s, A350s, MAXs, neos, 777-300ERs or A220s — and no 747s or A380s at any age, passenger or freighter. The biggest thing you can lease is a 777-200ER or an A330-300.

**You can only have so many leases on order at once** — five, or 25% of the aircraft you already have, whichever is larger. There's no limit on how many leased aircraft you can *fly*. The limit is how many can sit in the delivery queue together. Take delivery and the slots free up, so you grow in waves instead of ordering a hundred frames in one click.

**Fares are 5% lower**, passengers and freight alike.

**Crew wages rise 5% a year as your airline ages**, capped at 2.5× after twenty years — seniority, the way it works at a real airline. A startup pays starting rates; a twenty-year-old carrier carries a twenty-year-old payroll. Your pay slider still means what it always meant: relative to the going rate.

**Overhead is charged per departure, by aircraft class** — $200 for a turboprop up to $15,000 for a double-decker — rather than a flat rate per airframe. Big metal costs more to run an airline around, which it always should have.

Want something modern, or something large? Buy it. That's the whole point: growth should cost capital, not a deposit.

Every other world is untouched. This is per-world and entirely opt-in, and it only goes wider if it plays well.

What I want to know is whether it feels tight but fair, or just slow — especially from anyone trying long-haul, where the lease ceiling bites hardest.
